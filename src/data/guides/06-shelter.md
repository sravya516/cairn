# shelter-in-place-planning | Shelter-in-Place Planning
category: shelter
priority: critical
tags: shelter in place, stay home, hazardous materials, sealed room, lockdown
sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc
related: checklist:shelter-in-place, guide:ventilation-co-safety, guide:family-emergency-plan, guide:emergency-sleeping
summary: Sometimes the safest action is to stay where you are. Know when to shelter in place and how.

## Overview
Sheltering in place means staying inside a building and protecting yourself from outside hazards, rather than evacuating. Officials may order it for severe weather, chemical releases, nuclear or radiological events, civil unrest, disease outbreaks or when roads are unsafe.

## When to shelter in place
- You are told to by emergency officials.
- The danger is outside and moving would expose you (for example a chemical plume).
- Roads are blocked, flooded or icy.
- You are in a sturdy building during a tornado or severe storm.

## Steps for most hazards
1. Go inside right away. Bring pets, if possible.
2. Close and lock all windows and doors. Close fireplace dampers.
3. Turn off fans, heating and air conditioning, and close vents if told to shelter from outdoor contamination.
4. Go to an interior room with few or no windows, on the highest level for chemical vapors that sink, or the lowest level for storms.
5. Tune to official sources (radio, TV, alerts) and wait for instructions.
6. Call 911 only for a life-threatening emergency.

## Sealing a room for contamination
If told to seal a room: use plastic sheeting and duct tape over windows, doors and vents. A sealed room has limited air, so officials generally advise keeping it only for a short period. Do not stay sealed with a fuel-burning appliance.

## Supplies for the room
Water, food, flashlight, radio, first-aid kit, medications, phone and charger, cleaning wipes, toilet supplies, blankets and games for children.

## After
Do not leave until officials say it is safe. Open windows and ventilate when told that the danger has passed.

!! Shelter in place is not a reason to ignore carbon monoxide risks. Never run generators, grills or camp stoves in the shelter area.

# emergency-sleeping | Emergency Sleeping Arrangements
category: shelter
priority: normal
tags: sleeping, bedding, sleeping bags, cots, warmth, safe sleep
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, cdc
related: guide:staying-warm, guide:staying-cool, guide:shelter-in-place-planning
summary: Plan beds, insulation and safe sleep for adults, children and infants when the power is out or the home is damaged.

## Overview
Good sleep supports health and decisions. In a disaster you may sleep in a cold house, a shelter or a car.

## Steps
1. Choose the safest space: away from windows, damaged walls, falling trees or flooding.
2. Insulate from the ground with a sleeping pad, cot, air mattress or stacked blankets. Cold ground drains heat faster than cold air.
3. Use sleeping bags rated for the temperature, and layered blankets. Wear a hat.
4. In heat, sleep in the coolest room, use light sheets and keep hydrated.
5. Keep a flashlight, shoes, phone and whistle at the bedside.
6. Stay together in small groups to share heat in cold weather.

## Infants
- Follow safe sleep rules: baby on the back, in a crib or bassinet or a firm flat surface, with no loose blankets, pillows or toys. Avoid bed sharing.
- Dress infants in warm layers and use a wearable blanket, not loose bedding.

## In a shelter or car
- Keep valuables close and use the buddy system.
- Never sleep in a running car in a closed garage. Do not leave an engine idling in a car stuck in snow without keeping the tailpipe clear, because of carbon monoxide.

## Comfort
Earplugs and eye masks can help in noisy shelters. Keep a routine for children.

!! Do not use heating devices that could be a fire or carbon monoxide hazard near beds.

# indoor-temperature | Maintaining Safe Indoor Temperatures
category: shelter
priority: high
tags: indoor temperature, thermometer, cold home, hot home, thermal safety
sources: cdc, ready-gov, nws
related: guide:staying-warm, guide:staying-cool, guide:hypothermia, guide:heat-illness
summary: Know when indoor temperatures become dangerous and what to do about it.

## Overview
Homes can become dangerous without heating or cooling. Cold indoor temperatures cause hypothermia, and hot indoor temperatures cause heat illness. The risk is highest for infants, older adults, people with chronic health conditions and those who take certain medications.

## Watch the numbers
- Keep a thermometer where it is easy to read.
- Below about **64–65°F (18°C)** indoors is a concern for older adults and infants. Below about **60°F (15.5°C)** health risks climb for many people.
- Indoor temperatures above about **90°F (32°C)** with high humidity can be dangerous, especially for older adults.
- These are planning thresholds, not exact limits. People vary. Use judgment and seek help early.

## Actions
1. Check on vulnerable people at least a few times a day.
2. If it is too cold or hot and cannot be fixed, **leave** for a warming or cooling center, shelter, hotel or friend's home.
3. Gather in a smaller space and use layers (cold) or shade and cross-ventilation (heat).
4. Drink fluids and eat regularly.
5. Recognize symptoms and act: see Hypothermia and Heat Illness guides.

## When to leave
Leave if the temperature is outside a safe range and cannot be corrected within a few hours, if anyone develops symptoms, or if a vulnerable person is in the home.

!! Never use a gas oven, charcoal grill or portable generator indoors to warm or power a home. They can cause fatal carbon monoxide poisoning.

# staying-warm | Staying Warm Without Electricity
category: shelter
priority: high
tags: heating, warmth, layers, blankets, wood stove, safe heater, clothing
sources: cdc, nfpa, cpsc, ready-gov
related: guide:winter-storms, guide:hypothermia, guide:ventilation-co-safety
summary: Layer up, shrink your living space and use only safe heat sources with ventilation and alarms.

## Overview
Heat loss comes from conduction, convection, evaporation and radiation. The goal is to retain body heat and heat a small space safely.

## Steps
1. Wear **layers**: a base layer that wicks moisture, an insulating layer (wool, fleece) and a wind-resistant outer layer. Add a hat, socks and gloves.
2. Keep clothes dry. Wet clothing loses heat quickly.
3. Close off rooms, block drafts with towels, hang blankets over windows and doors.
4. Gather in one room. Use sleeping bags and blankets. Body heat from several people warms a small room.
5. Eat regularly and drink warm fluids. Food provides body heat.
6. Stay active but avoid sweating.
7. Use hot water bottles with safe water, wrapped in cloth.

## Safe heat sources
- Fixtures designed for indoor use, such as a properly installed and maintained fireplace, wood stove, or vented kerosene heater, if permitted and used per the manufacturer's directions.
- Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet away from heaters.
- Have a working fire extinguisher and smoke and carbon monoxide alarms with batteries.

## Never do this
- Never use a charcoal grill, camp stove, gas oven or generator indoors.
- Never leave heaters or candles unattended or while sleeping.
- Never use an outdoor-only heater in an enclosed space.

!! Carbon monoxide kills people who sleep through it. Use battery-operated CO alarms, and if the alarm sounds, go outside and call 911.

# staying-cool | Staying Cool During Heat Waves
category: shelter
priority: high
tags: cooling, heat wave, fans, shade, cooling center, evaporative cooling
sources: cdc, nws, ready-gov
related: guide:extreme-heat, guide:heat-illness, guide:indoor-temperature
summary: Reduce heat at home, cool the body directly and move to a cooler place when you cannot cool your home.

## Overview
When the power is out, you may have to cool yourself and your home without air conditioning. Cooling your body is more efficient than cooling a house.

## Steps
1. Go to a cooling center, library, mall or friend's home if you can.
2. Close blinds and curtains on sunny windows during the day. Use reflective sheeting or light-colored shades.
3. Open windows at night or when it is cooler outside and use cross-ventilation.
4. Stay on the lowest floor in the coolest room.
5. Cool your body: take cool showers or baths, use wet towels on your neck, wrists and head, and wet your clothes.
6. Wear light, loose clothing. Rest in shade.
7. Drink water or oral rehydration solutions regularly and eat light meals.
8. Avoid using the oven or stove during the day.

## Fans
Fans help evaporate sweat, but when it is very hot (above the mid-90s °F, about 35°C) and humid, fans alone may not prevent heat illness. Use fans with wet skin and other cooling measures.

## Check on others
Check on older adults, infants and people with chronic illness often. Never leave anyone in a closed car.

## Warning signs
Dizziness, headache, nausea, confusion or fainting: act at once. See the Heat Illness guide.

!! Heat stroke is an emergency. Call 911 and cool the person while you wait.

# emergency-toilets-waste | Emergency Toilets and Waste Management
category: shelter
priority: critical
tags: toilet, sanitation, bucket toilet, human waste, garbage, latrine, sewage
sources: cdc, epa, ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:handwashing-hygiene, guide:cleaning-sanitation, checklist:kit-2w
summary: When sewer or water service fails, set up a safe emergency toilet and manage waste to prevent disease.

## Overview
Human waste spreads disease quickly. Plan a safe toilet system before you need one. Do not keep flushing if sewer lines may be damaged or water is off.

## Bucket toilet system
1. Use a sturdy 5-gallon bucket with a snap-on lid, or a toilet with a plastic bag liner (a sturdy trash bag or a double bag).
2. Add a layer of absorbent material (cat litter, sawdust, wood shavings, or commercial gelling powder) in the bottom.
3. Add a toilet seat if you have one. A pool noodle cut open can make a seat.
4. After each use, add a scoop of absorbent material and close the lid.
5. When the bag is about half full, tie it and place it inside a second bag, then store it in a covered container away from living areas until it can be disposed of per local guidance.
6. Wash your hands with soap and clean water, or use hand sanitizer.
7. Clean the bucket and seat with a disinfectant.

## Other tips
- If your toilet works but water is off, you may flush with a bucket of non-drinking water into the bowl when sewer lines are intact. Check with officials.
- If sewer lines are damaged, do not use the toilet or flush.
- Keep a supply of heavy-duty bags, absorbent, wipes, toilet paper, bleach and gloves.
- Do not bury waste near water sources, wells or gardens. If burying is unavoidable (an outdoor latrine), dig at least 200 feet from water, in a place that won't flood, about 6–8 inches deep, away from camp, and cover with soil.

## Garbage
Bag all trash, tie it, and store it in sealed containers away from living areas to avoid pests. Burn or bury only where local authorities say it is safe.

!! Do not dump waste into storm drains or water sources. Wear gloves and wash hands after handling waste.

# handwashing-hygiene | Handwashing and Personal Hygiene
category: shelter
priority: high
tags: handwashing, hygiene, soap, hand sanitizer, bathing, hand hygiene
sources: cdc, red-cross
related: guide:infection-prevention-hygiene, guide:emergency-toilets-waste
summary: Handwashing is the single most effective way to stop illness. Stretch limited water with a simple handwashing station.

## Overview
Diarrheal and respiratory illnesses spread fast in emergencies. Clean hands protect you and your family.

## When to wash
After using the toilet or changing diapers, before eating or preparing food, after handling garbage or cleaning, after contact with floodwater, after caring for someone who is sick, after touching pets and after coughing or sneezing.

## How to wash
1. Wet hands with safe water.
2. Lather with soap, covering all surfaces, for at least 20 seconds.
3. Rinse and dry with a clean towel or air dry.

## Low-water handwashing station
1. Hang a jug with a small hole, or use a bottle with a spigot.
2. Use a bucket underneath to collect the used water.
3. Keep soap and a clean towel nearby.
4. Use one tablespoon or less of water to wet, and rinse with a small stream.
5. Dispose of wash water away from drinking sources.

## When water is limited
Use hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol on hands that are not visibly dirty. Wipes help for dirt but are not a substitute for handwashing when possible.

## Personal hygiene
- Sponge-bathe with a small amount of water: face, underarms, groin, feet.
- Brush teeth with safe water.
- Change clothes and underwear regularly.
- Keep nails short and clean. Use gloves for cleanup.
- Women and girls: stock menstrual products.

>> Include soap, hand sanitizer, wipes, toothbrushes and toothpaste in every kit.

# cleaning-sanitation | Cleaning, Sanitation, Pests and Emergency Laundry
category: shelter
priority: normal
tags: cleaning, disinfecting, pest control, laundry, mold, rodents, sanitation
sources: cdc, epa, ready-gov
related: guide:infection-prevention-hygiene, guide:emergency-toilets-waste, guide:flooding
summary: Keep your living space clean, deter pests and wash clothes with minimal water and no power.

## Overview
Clean surfaces and food areas reduce disease. Disrupted garbage collection and standing water attract pests.

## Cleaning and disinfecting
1. Clean with soap and water first, then disinfect. Disinfectant works poorly on dirt.
2. For a bleach solution for surfaces, use unscented household bleach per the label and EPA or CDC guidance, make it fresh and use it in a ventilated place. Allow the proper contact time.
3. Never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar or other cleaners.
4. Wear gloves and eye protection. Keep chemicals away from children and food.
5. Clean food preparation surfaces and utensils before each use.

## Pests
- Keep food in sealed containers and clean up crumbs.
- Bag and seal garbage and keep it away from the house.
- Remove standing water to reduce mosquitoes.
- Seal gaps where rodents can enter. Use traps rather than poisons around children and pets.

## Emergency laundry
1. Fill a bucket with water and a small amount of detergent. Agitate with a clean plunger or stick.
2. Soak, scrub and wring out. Rinse in a second bucket.
3. Hang to dry in sun and air.
4. For contaminated clothing (for example after flood cleanup), bag it separately and wash with hot water and detergent when possible.
5. Prioritize underwear, socks and base layers.

## Mold
After flooding or leaks, dry out within 24 to 48 hours. Wear an N95 respirator, gloves and goggles for cleanup. Discard porous materials that were soaked.

!! Do not use bleach and other chemicals in closed rooms. Ventilate.

# ventilation-co-safety | Ventilation, Combustion and Carbon Monoxide Safety
category: shelter
priority: critical
tags: carbon monoxide, CO, ventilation, generator, combustion, alarm, poisoning
sources: cdc, cpsc, nfpa, ready-gov
related: guide:generator-safety, guide:cooking-no-power, guide:staying-warm, guide:poisoning
summary: Carbon monoxide is invisible and kills quickly. Never run fuel-burning equipment indoors or in garages, and use battery CO alarms.

## Overview
Carbon monoxide (CO) is an odorless, colorless gas produced by burning fuel. Every year people die from CO poisoning during power outages and cold weather because they use generators, grills, camp stoves, or heaters indoors or in a partially enclosed space.

## The rules
1. **Generators run outdoors only**, at least 20 feet (6 meters) from the house, with the exhaust pointing away from doors, windows and vents. Never in a garage, even with the door open, and never in a basement, crawl space or shed.
2. Never use charcoal or gas grills, camp stoves or propane heaters designed for outdoor use inside any building, tent, or vehicle.
3. Never use a gas oven or stove burners to heat your home.
4. Do not idle a vehicle in a garage, even with the door open.
5. Keep snow clear from vehicle tailpipes, furnace and dryer vents, and exhaust pipes.
6. Have fuel-burning appliances, chimneys and vents inspected yearly.
7. Use only indoor-rated heaters, following directions and clearances.

## Alarms
- Install **battery-powered or battery backup CO alarms** on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas.
- Test them monthly and replace batteries. Replace alarms according to the manufacturer's date, typically every 5–10 years.
- Keep spares in the kit.

## Symptoms
Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea or vomiting, shortness of breath, confusion, chest pain, and blurred vision. People who are asleep or drunk may die before symptoms appear.

## If the alarm sounds or you suspect CO
1. Get everyone outside to fresh air immediately. Do not stop to gather things.
2. Call 911 from outside.
3. Do not re-enter until emergency responders say it is safe.
4. Seek medical care for everyone who was exposed.

!! Opening windows or running a fan does not make it safe to run a generator indoors.
