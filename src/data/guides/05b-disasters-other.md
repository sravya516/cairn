# wildfires | Wildfires
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: wildfire, smoke, defensible space, evacuation, N95, ember, ready set go
sources: ready-gov, fema, usfs, cdc, nws
related: checklist:wildfire, guide:evacuation-planning, guide:go-bag, guide:flash-floods
summary: Prepare your home and a go-bag, leave early when told and protect your lungs from smoke.

## Understanding the threat
Wildfires can spread faster than people can run and can throw burning embers miles ahead of the fire front, igniting homes. Smoke can harm health hundreds of miles away. After fires, burned slopes are at risk for flash floods and debris flows.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Create defensible space. Clear dead vegetation, leaves and pine needles within 5 feet of the house first, then reduce fuel in the zones beyond. Follow your local fire agency's guidance.
2. Harden your home: use non-combustible roofing, cover vents with fine metal mesh, clean gutters, and keep woodpiles and propane tanks away from structures.
3. Know at least two ways out of your neighborhood.
4. Prepare a go-bag per person with N95 or similar respirators, documents, medications and chargers.
5. Plan for pets and livestock.
6. Sign up for local alerts.
7. Keep your vehicle fueled and pointed outward when parked at home.

## Supplies required
Go-bags, N95 respirators (not cloth or surgical masks), eye protection, long cotton or wool clothing, sturdy shoes, water, first-aid kit, medications, radio, phone chargers, cash, documents, and copies of insurance.

## Warning signs
Red flag or fire weather warnings, smoke or glow on the horizon, falling ash, official evacuation notices (warnings and orders). Do not wait to see flames.

## During the event
1. **Leave immediately when told to evacuate.** Follow designated routes.
2. If you have time, close all windows and doors, remove flammable curtains, move furniture to the center, turn off gas, and turn on outdoor lights.
3. Wear protective clothing and a respirator if smoke is present.
4. If trapped, call 911, stay in your car or a clear area and cover up with blankets. Do not run through flames.
5. Avoid driving through heavy smoke if possible and use headlights.

## Evacuation considerations
"Ready, Set, Go": be ready early, set your supplies when conditions worsen, go when told. Tell someone your destination. Bring pets in carriers. Expect traffic and closures.

## Shelter considerations
Use evacuation shelters, hotels or friends away from the fire and smoke. Do not shelter in place in the path of an advancing fire unless officials advise it.

## Afterward
1. Do not return until officials say it is safe.
2. Beware of hot spots, ash pits, hazardous trees and damaged utilities.
3. Wear N95 respirators, gloves, boots and eye protection during cleanup. Avoid stirring ash.
4. Discard food exposed to heat, smoke or lack of refrigeration.
5. Be aware of flash flood risk on burned slopes.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting for an order that may not arrive in time.
- Using cloth or surgical masks for smoke.
- Failing to prepare pets and livestock.
- Ignoring smoke health effects on children and those with heart or lung disease.

## Special considerations for families
Smoke harms young children, pregnant people, older adults and people with heart or lung disease the most. Use a clean room with a HEPA filter, or a DIY box-fan filter, and keep windows closed. Practice evacuation with children.

# earthquakes | Earthquakes
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: earthquake, drop cover hold on, aftershock, tsunami, shaking, secure furniture
sources: usgs, ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: guide:go-bag, guide:emergency-documents, guide:shelter-in-place-planning
summary: Drop, Cover and Hold On at the first sign of shaking, and prepare for aftershocks and utility failures.

## Understanding the threat
Earthquakes strike without warning. Most injuries occur from falling objects, collapsing structures and broken glass. Aftershocks can continue for days. Earthquakes can trigger tsunamis, landslides, fires and utility failures.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Secure heavy furniture, bookcases, water heaters, TVs and hanging objects to walls.
2. Store heavy items low and breakables in closed cabinets with latches.
3. Identify safe spots in each room (under sturdy tables, away from windows and heavy furniture).
4. Know how to turn off gas, water and electricity. Keep a wrench for the gas valve if directed by your utility.
5. Prepare 2-week supplies if you live in an earthquake zone, since services can be disrupted for a long time.
6. Practice "Drop, Cover and Hold On".
7. Learn your area's building and tsunami risk and routes.

## Supplies required
Water, food, flashlights, radio, whistle, first-aid kit, sturdy shoes by the bed, gloves, fire extinguisher, cash, medications, and 2 weeks of supplies if you live in a high-risk area.

## Warning signs
Usually none. Some regions have early-warning alerts that give seconds of notice. Near coasts, strong or long shaking (20 seconds or more) or an official warning is a signal to move to high ground.

## During the event
1. **Drop** to hands and knees. **Cover** your head and neck under a sturdy table or desk, or against an interior wall away from windows. **Hold On** until the shaking stops.
2. If in bed, stay there and protect your head with a pillow.
3. Do not run outside, and do not stand in a doorway.
4. If outdoors, move to an open area away from buildings, trees, streetlights and power lines.
5. If driving, pull over in a clear area, stay in the vehicle with the seat belt fastened. Avoid bridges, overpasses and underpasses.
6. If you use a wheelchair, lock the wheels and cover your head and neck.

## Evacuation considerations
After shaking stops, check for injuries and hazards. If you are near the coast and the shaking was strong or lasted 20 seconds or more, or if you receive a tsunami warning, immediately move inland and to high ground on foot. Do not wait for official alerts.

## Shelter considerations
Remain outdoors in open space if your building is damaged. Return only if officials say it is safe. Expect aftershocks.

## Afterward
1. Check yourself and others for injuries. Give first aid.
2. Smell for gas. If you smell gas or hear hissing, leave, and call from outside. Turn off the gas only if you suspect a leak and know how.
3. Check for fire, damaged wiring, and water lines. Do not use matches or lighters.
4. Wear sturdy shoes to avoid broken glass.
5. Use text messages and keep calls short.
6. Expect aftershocks and repeat Drop, Cover and Hold On.
7. Photograph damage for insurance.

## Common mistakes
- Running outside during shaking.
- Standing in doorways.
- Using elevators after the quake.
- Lighting candles or matches near a gas leak.
- Ignoring the tsunami risk near a coast.

## Special considerations for families
Teach children to drop, cover and hold on. Practice drills. Keep shoes and flashlights near beds. Plan how you will reunite if children are at school, and know the school's earthquake plan.

# extreme-heat | Extreme Heat
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: heat wave, heat index, cooling center, heat advisory, hot weather
sources: nws, cdc, ready-gov, osha
related: guide:heat-illness, guide:staying-cool, guide:dehydration
summary: Heat kills more people than most other weather events. Stay cool, drink water and check on vulnerable people.

## Understanding the threat
Extreme heat is a period of unusually hot weather, often with high humidity. The heat index combines temperature and humidity to show how hot it feels. Heat waves can cause heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and can worsen heart and lung disease. Urban areas are hotter at night.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Know where you can cool down: air-conditioned buildings, libraries, community cooling centers.
2. Service air conditioning and check window shades, fans and insulation.
3. Stock water and oral rehydration salts, if relevant.
4. Learn the signs of heat illness.
5. Identify neighbors, older adults and people with health conditions to check on.
6. Plan outdoor work and exercise for cooler times.

## Supplies required
Water, fans (battery-powered if possible), light-colored loose clothing, hats, sunscreen, thermometers, window coverings, spray bottles, a way to keep food cold.

## Warning signs
Heat advisories, excessive heat watches or warnings from the NWS; a heat index in the high 90s or higher; poor air quality; hot nights with little cooling.

## During the event
1. Stay in air-conditioned places as much as possible. Even a few hours a day helps.
2. Drink water often, regardless of thirst. Avoid alcohol and very sugary drinks.
3. Wear lightweight, light-colored, loose clothing and a hat.
4. Use cool showers or wet cloths.
5. Avoid strenuous activity in the heat, especially in the afternoon.
6. **Never leave children, pets or anyone in a parked car,** even briefly.
7. Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke and act quickly.

## Evacuation considerations
If your home is dangerously hot and has no power, go to a cooling center, public building or relative's home. Do not wait for symptoms.

## Shelter considerations
Choose the coolest room, close blinds in the day, open windows at night when it is cooler, and use fans carefully. Fans do not prevent heat illness at very high temperatures, as they only move hot air.

## Afterward
Continue to hydrate and watch for lingering symptoms. Check on others, and plan for the next heat wave.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting until you are thirsty.
- Relying on fans alone during extreme heat.
- Leaving pets or children in cars.
- Doing intense outdoor work at midday.
- Not checking on isolated neighbors.

## Special considerations for families
Infants and young children overheat quickly. Dress them lightly, give fluids as appropriate and never leave them in vehicles. Older adults may not feel thirst or heat, and some medications raise heat risk. Pets need shade, water and cool places.

# extreme-cold | Extreme Cold
category: disasters
priority: high
tags: cold wave, wind chill, frostbite, frozen pipes, cold snap, warming
sources: nws, cdc, ready-gov
related: guide:hypothermia, guide:winter-storms, guide:staying-warm
summary: Dress in layers, protect pipes, heat safely and watch for hypothermia and frostbite.

## Understanding the threat
Extreme cold and high wind chill can cause frostbite in minutes and hypothermia over longer periods. Cold homes during power outages are a leading cause of preventable cold deaths. Using unsafe heaters causes fires and carbon monoxide poisoning.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Insulate pipes, seal drafts and know how to shut off water.
2. Service your furnace, chimney and heating equipment.
3. Install smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.
4. Stock warm clothing, blankets, sleeping bags and hot food options.
5. Keep your vehicle's winter kit and gas tank full.
6. Plan to check on neighbors and pets.

## Supplies required
Warm layers (wool or synthetic), insulated boots, hats, gloves, blankets, sleeping bags rated for cold, emergency blankets, safe heating equipment, thermometers, a battery radio, flashlights and food.

## Warning signs
Wind chill advisories or warnings, temperatures well below freezing, forecasts of storms with outages.

## During the event
1. Stay indoors if possible. Limit time outside.
2. Dress in several loose layers, with a wind-resistant outer layer, and cover your head, face, hands and feet.
3. Keep moving and eat enough.
4. Keep one room warm. Close doors to unused rooms.
5. Let faucets drip if pipes might freeze, and open cabinet doors.
6. Use only approved heaters, keep them 3 feet from anything that burns, never leave them unattended, and never use stoves or ovens for heat.
7. Watch for hypothermia and frostbite.

## Evacuation considerations
If your home loses heat for long periods, relocate to a warming center, shelter or friend's house. Do not wait until it is dangerously cold inside.

## Shelter considerations
Gather in a small, well-insulated room. Use blankets and sleeping bags. Hang blankets over windows. Do not block vents on fuel-burning appliances.

## Afterward
Check pipes, roof, vehicles and neighbors. Thaw frozen pipes slowly using safe methods such as a hair dryer, never an open flame.

## Common mistakes
- Using generators or grills indoors.
- Ignoring early frostbite signs.
- Wearing cotton in wet cold.
- Using a gas oven for heat.
- Leaving pets outside.

## Special considerations for families
Dress infants warmly but safely. Avoid heavy blankets in cribs. Babies and older adults lose heat quickly. Check on older relatives living alone.

# landslides | Landslides and Debris Flows
category: disasters
priority: normal
tags: landslide, mudslide, debris flow, slope, burn scar, rockfall
sources: usgs, ready-gov, fema
related: guide:flash-floods, guide:wildfires, guide:earthquakes
summary: Learn the signs of slope failure, avoid steep areas in heavy rain and move away from the slide path.

## Understanding the threat
Landslides happen when rock, soil or debris moves down a slope. They are triggered by heavy rain, rapid snowmelt, earthquakes, volcanic activity, wildfires and human activity. Debris flows can move fast and carry boulders and trees.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Learn about landslide risk in your area from local geological resources and the USGS.
2. Avoid building or living at the base of steep slopes, near the mouth of canyons or at the edge of cliffs.
3. Plant ground cover and ensure good drainage around your home.
4. Know your evacuation route and a safe location away from slopes.
5. Pay attention to burned slopes after wildfires.

## Supplies required
Go-bag, radio, flashlights, whistle, sturdy shoes, first-aid kit, water, and a plan to leave quickly.

## Warning signs
New cracks in the ground, pavement or walls; tilting trees, fences or poles; doors and windows that stick; sudden changes in stream water level or muddiness; rumbling sound; bulges at the base of slopes; springs or wet spots on slopes.

## During the event
1. If you hear or see signs, **leave immediately**. Move away from the path of the slide, to high ground perpendicular to the flow, if possible.
2. During intense rain, stay alert and consider leaving before the worst.
3. If you cannot leave, move to the upper floor of a sturdy building on the side away from the slope.
4. If caught in a slide, curl into a tight ball and protect your head.
5. Do not cross a road that has debris or water flowing over it.

## Evacuation considerations
Evacuate when ordered, and when conditions are risky. Warn neighbors. Leave your home if it shows signs of ground movement.

## Shelter considerations
Avoid sheltering downhill from steep slopes. Choose shelters out of the slide path.

## Afterward
1. Stay away from the slide area, since additional slides are possible.
2. Check for injured people near the slide, without entering the slide area. Call for help.
3. Watch for broken utilities and flooding.
4. Report damage to authorities and have a professional examine the ground and structure before returning.

## Common mistakes
- Ignoring early ground cracks.
- Returning too soon.
- Camping in canyon bottoms or below slopes in rainy weather.
- Driving through debris on roads.

## Special considerations for families
Teach children to stay away from steep, wet slopes. Make sure schools or campsites have evacuation plans. Consider leaving early with children and pets when heavy rain is forecast and your area is at high risk.

# drought | Drought
category: disasters
priority: normal
tags: drought, water shortage, water conservation, wildfire risk, restrictions
sources: usgs, epa, fema, ready-gov
related: guide:water-storage, guide:wildfires, guide:gardening-food-security
summary: Droughts develop slowly but can reduce water supplies, raise fire risk and affect food prices and health.

## Understanding the threat
Drought is a prolonged period of low rainfall. It affects water supply, agriculture, wildlife and fire danger. Private wells may run dry. Water restrictions may be imposed. Dust and heat can worsen health problems.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Check for leaks and repair them. Install water-efficient fixtures and appliances.
2. Learn local water restrictions and follow them.
3. Plant drought-tolerant landscaping and use mulch.
4. Know the condition and depth of your well if you have one, and have backup water storage.
5. Create defensible space around your home for fire safety.
6. Keep stored emergency water in rotation.

## Supplies required
Stored water, rain barrels where permitted, buckets for greywater reuse where allowed, and hoses with shutoff nozzles.

## Warning signs
Low reservoir levels, official drought declarations, dry soil and dead vegetation, low streams, lower water pressure, wells running dry.

## During the event
1. Conserve water: shorter showers, full loads only, turn off taps, and fix leaks.
2. Water outdoor plants early or late and use drip irrigation. Follow restrictions.
3. Reuse household water where allowed (for example, rinse water for plants).
4. Keep a watch on fire danger. Avoid outdoor burning and equipment that sparks.
5. Check on farm animals' water and forage.
6. Stay hydrated during hot dry weather.

## Evacuation considerations
Drought itself rarely requires evacuation, but severe water shortages or wildfire may. Plan for a relocation option if your well fails.

## Shelter considerations
If you lose water at home, use stored water for drinking and sanitation. Contact local authorities about distribution sites and water quality.

## Afterward
Rainfall may bring flash floods on dry, hard soils and burn scars. Keep conserving, because recovery from drought takes time.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting for severe restrictions to begin conserving.
- Ignoring slow leaks.
- Drinking from unsafe sources if wells fail.
- Overlooking fire risk.

## Special considerations for families
Teach children conservation habits. Keep hydration a priority for kids and older adults in dry heat. People who rely on well water should test it regularly and ask local health departments about risks during low water.

# extended-power-outages | Extended Power Outages
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: power outage, blackout, grid failure, outage, utility, generator, food safety
sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc, cpsc, usda
related: checklist:power-outage, guide:power-outage-planning, guide:generator-safety, guide:food-safety-outage, guide:ventilation-co-safety
summary: Plan for light, communication, heating or cooling, food safety, medical devices and carbon monoxide safety during outages lasting days.

## Understanding the threat
Power outages come from storms, heat waves, equipment failure, wildfires, planned shutoffs and attacks on the grid. Long outages affect refrigeration, heating and cooling, water systems, communications, fuel pumps and medical devices.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Create an outage kit: flashlights, lanterns, batteries, radio, power banks, and a first-aid kit.
2. Know how to open an electric garage door manually.
3. Keep your vehicle's tank at least half full.
4. Have a plan for medical devices and refrigerated medications.
5. Install battery-powered smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.
6. Keep cash. Card readers and ATMs may be down.
7. Know whom to contact: your utility's outage line (by phone or text) and the nearest emergency shelter.

## Supplies required
Light sources, batteries, radio, power bank or station, water, non-perishable food and a manual can opener, coolers and ice, warm or cool-down supplies, cash, and a safe way to heat or cook outdoors.

## Warning signs
Storm or heat wave forecasts, utility notices of planned shutoffs, flickering lights, and reports of widespread outages.

## During the event
1. Report your outage to your utility and check whether neighbors are affected.
2. Use flashlights, not candles, to reduce fire risk.
3. Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed (see Food Safety During Power Outages).
4. Unplug sensitive electronics and keep one light switched on to see when power returns.
5. Stay away from downed power lines. Assume they are live and call 911 and the utility.
6. **Never use a generator, grill, camp stove or heater indoors or in an attached garage.** Keep generators at least 20 feet from the home with exhaust pointed away.
7. Conserve phone battery. Turn on low-power mode and text.
8. Stay warm or cool safely. Dress in layers or seek cool places.

## Evacuation considerations
Move to a shelter or friend's home if conditions are unsafe: extreme temperatures, medical equipment needs, or a very long outage. Plan before the outage and tell someone.

## Shelter considerations
If staying home, choose one room to heat or cool. Use safe equipment only. Check on neighbors, especially older adults.

## Afterward
1. Turn appliances back on gradually to prevent overloads.
2. Check food using USDA guidelines and discard perishable food above 40°F for over 2 hours.
3. Restock supplies and note what you needed.
4. Check generators and fuel storage.

## Common mistakes
- Running generators in garages or close to windows.
- Opening the refrigerator repeatedly.
- Using candles unattended.
- Not having a plan for medical equipment.
- Letting phones die early.

## Special considerations for families
Keep children warm or cool and entertained with games and books. Have a plan for infants' formula and feeding without power. Protect older adults and people who rely on electric medical devices. Keep pets safe from cold and heat.
