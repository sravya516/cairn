# food-storage-basics | Emergency Food Storage Basics
category: food
priority: critical
tags: food storage, short-term, long-term, shelf-stable, temperature, calories
sources: ready-gov, usda, fema, red-cross
related: checklist:food-storage, calc:food, guide:pantry-staples, guide:food-rotation
summary: Build a pantry of foods you will actually eat that stays safe without refrigeration, and store it correctly.

## Overview
Emergency food should be shelf-stable, require little or no cooking, and suit everyone in your household. The best food plan is one you already eat daily, so it stays fresh through rotation.

## Short-term versus long-term
- **Short-term (3–14 days):** ready-to-eat foods that need little or no cooking and water: canned meats, fish, fruit, vegetables, soups, peanut butter, crackers, granola bars, dried fruit, nuts, shelf-stable milk, cereal, juice boxes, comfort foods.
- **Long-term (weeks to months):** bulk staples such as white rice, dried beans, pasta, rolled oats, flour, sugar, salt, cooking oil, powdered milk, canned goods, freeze-dried meals. These need water and cooking fuel.

## How much
- A common planning figure is about 2,000 calories per adult per day, less for young children and more for hard work, cold weather or breastfeeding. Use the Food Calculator for your household.
- Plan for 3 meals a day plus snacks, and extra for morale.
- Include water-efficient options, since food that needs lots of water uses your water supply.

## Storage conditions
1. Cool, dry, dark places are best. Heat shortens shelf life. Around 50–70°F (10–21°C) is ideal; avoid places above 85°F (29°C), such as attics and uninsulated garages.
2. Keep food off the floor in sealed, pest-proof containers.
3. Keep food away from chemicals, fuel and moisture.
4. Store a manual can opener, utensils, plates and cleaning supplies with it.
5. Label items with purchase dates and rotate (see Food Rotation).

## Food safety basics
- Do not eat canned food from cans that are bulging, leaking, deeply dented, or rusted through.
- Discard food with odd smells, mold or spoiled appearance. When in doubt, throw it out.
- Wash hands and utensils; use safe water.

>> Buy two of what you already eat each shopping trip. The extra goes into the emergency pantry.

# pantry-staples | Canned Foods, Dry Goods, Rice, Beans, Pasta and Grains
category: food
priority: high
tags: canned food, dry goods, rice, beans, pasta, grains, staples, shelf life
sources: usda, ready-gov, cdc
related: guide:food-storage-basics, guide:food-rotation, guide:emergency-meal-planning
summary: What to stock, how long each lasts, and how to store it so it stays safe and tasty.

## Overview
These everyday staples form the backbone of an affordable emergency pantry. Shelf life depends on storage conditions. The numbers below are general guidelines, not guarantees.

## Canned foods
- High-acid canned foods (tomatoes, fruit, juices) are best for quality within roughly 12–18 months. Low-acid canned foods (meat, poultry, fish, most vegetables) are best for quality within about 2–5 years.
- Canned food can often remain safe long after the best-by date if the can is undamaged and stored cool, but quality declines.
- Choose pop-top cans or keep a manual opener. Choose low-sodium options where needed.

## Dry goods
- **White rice** stores for years when kept dry, cool and sealed in pest-proof containers. Brown rice has oils that go rancid faster.
- **Dried beans, lentils and split peas** last for years but get harder over time. Older beans take longer to cook.
- **Pasta** keeps for a long time if dry and sealed.
- **Rolled oats, flour, cornmeal:** whole grains go stale faster; store sealed and cool.
- **Sugar, salt, honey:** last indefinitely when kept dry.
- **Cooking oil:** goes rancid. Rotate within about a year.
- **Peanut butter, nuts, dried fruit, crackers, granola bars:** rotate every 6–12 months.

## Cooking needs
Dry goods need water and fuel. Beans need long soaking and cooking. Plan for a pressure cooker, thermos cooking, or pre-cooked options if fuel is limited.

## Pest-proofing
1. Freeze new bags of flour or grain for several days before storing, if possible.
2. Store in food-grade buckets with gasket lids, glass jars or mylar bags.
3. Keep containers off the floor and check regularly.

!! Do not store large amounts of food in garages or sheds in hot climates. Heat and pests ruin it.

# freeze-dried-food | Freeze-Dried and Long-Shelf-Life Foods
category: food
priority: normal
tags: freeze-dried, dehydrated, long shelf life, mylar, oxygen absorbers
sources: usda, ready-gov
related: guide:pantry-staples, guide:food-rotation, guide:water-needs-calculation
summary: Understand the pros, cons and water needs of freeze-dried and commercial emergency food kits.

## Overview
Freeze-dried meals are lightweight and can last 20 years or more when sealed and stored cool, according to manufacturers. They are convenient for go-bags and for people who cannot cook much, but cost more per calorie.

## Advantages
- Very long shelf life when unopened, with low weight.
- Quick preparation, often just hot water.
- Good for evacuations and go-bags.

## Limits
- Need clean water (often 1–2 cups per meal). Count that in your water plan.
- Often high in sodium and low in calories per serving. Check the actual calories per package, and how many servings it really contains.
- Opened pouches spoil quickly. Reseal and use fast.
- Quality is not a substitute for a pantry you rotate.

## Buying and storing
1. Compare calories per dollar and per pound, and check for allergens.
2. Look for sealed, intact packaging with a clear date.
3. Taste-test one meal before buying many.
4. Store in a cool, dry place away from heat, light and moisture.
5. Do not rely on them as your only food. Combine with pantry staples.

## Do-it-yourself long-term packing
Dry staples (rice, beans, oats) can be sealed in mylar bags with oxygen absorbers inside buckets. Do not seal moist, high-fat or low-acid foods like this: botulism risk. Follow current USDA or university extension guidance.

!! Do not eat food from bulging or damaged pouches and cans.

# food-rotation | Food Rotation and Shelf Life
category: food
priority: high
tags: rotation, FIFO, expiration, best by, shelf life, inventory
sources: usda, ready-gov
related: guide:food-storage-basics, guide:pantry-staples, checklist:food-storage
summary: Use first-in, first-out rotation and understand what dates on food labels actually mean.

## Overview
Rotation keeps emergency food fresh and prevents waste. The rule is first in, first out (FIFO): eat the oldest first and put new items at the back.

## What dates mean
- **Best if used by / best before:** about quality, not safety. Food may be safe afterwards but may taste worse.
- **Use by:** the last date for peak quality of the product; follow it for infant formula and perishable items.
- **Sell by:** for the store, not you.
- Infant formula should never be used past its date.
- Home-canned or sealed foods: follow the guidance in the Food Preservation guide.

## Set up a rotation system
1. Write the purchase or expiration date on each item or box with a marker.
2. Store new items behind or beneath older ones.
3. Keep an inventory list (on paper or in this app's checklists) with quantities and dates.
4. Every month, check one shelf or bin and move near-expiry items to the kitchen.
5. Replace what you use on the next shopping trip.
6. Twice a year, do a full review along with water and batteries.

## Spoilage checks
- Cans: no bulges, leaks or rust through, deep dents on seams.
- Dry goods: no mold, moisture, bugs or musty smell.
- Oils and nuts: smell for rancid odor.
- Remember: when in doubt, throw it out.

>> Rotate with a "shop from the pantry" habit: use shelf-stable items in regular meals.

# food-safety-outage | Food Safety During Power Outages
category: food
priority: critical
tags: refrigerator, freezer, power outage, food safety, 40 degrees, spoilage, cooler
sources: usda, cdc, ready-gov
related: guide:refrigeration-outages, guide:power-outage-planning, checklist:power-outage
summary: Know how long refrigerated and frozen food stays safe, and what to throw out.

## Overview
Foods that need refrigeration can become dangerous when they sit above 40°F (4°C) for more than two hours. USDA and CDC guidance gives clear time limits.

## Key times
- **Refrigerator:** food stays safe for about **4 hours** if the door stays closed.
- **Full freezer:** about **48 hours** if closed; **half-full freezer:** about **24 hours**.
- **Above 40°F (4°C) for 2 hours or more** (1 hour above 90°F or 32°C): discard perishable food.

## Before an outage
1. Keep an appliance thermometer in the fridge and freezer. Refrigerator should be at or below 40°F (4°C), freezer at or below 0°F (-18°C).
2. Freeze water in containers and gel packs to keep cold.
3. Group freezer items to hold cold longer.
4. Know where you can get dry or block ice.
5. Keep coolers ready.

## During an outage
1. Keep doors closed. Open only when necessary.
2. Move essential items into coolers with ice if the outage will last more than 4 hours.
3. Use up ready-to-eat shelf-stable food first.
4. Use a thermometer to check food.

## After power returns
- Check each item. Discard perishable food that was above 40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours.
- Frozen food that still has ice crystals or is at 40°F (4°C) or below can be refrozen, though quality may drop.
- Do not taste-test to decide.
- Throw out food that touched floodwater, except for sealed, undamaged cans that have been washed and sanitized.

!! Never eat food that was exposed to floodwater unless it is in an undamaged, sealed can or pouch that has been properly cleaned and disinfected. Never use a generator, camp stove or charcoal grill indoors.

# cooking-no-power | Cooking Without Electricity and Portable Cooking Equipment
category: food
priority: critical
tags: camp stove, grill, cooking, carbon monoxide, fuel, outdoor cooking, solar oven
sources: cdc, cpsc, nfpa, ready-gov
related: guide:ventilation-co-safety, guide:fuel-storage-safety, guide:emergency-meal-planning
summary: Cook safely with outdoor-rated equipment and never bring fuel-burning devices indoors.

## Overview
Without power you can still cook, but the equipment changes and so do the risks. The largest hazard is carbon monoxide, an odorless gas that kills people every year after storms and outages.

## Options
- **Camp stoves (propane or butane canister, liquid fuel):** compact and quick. Use outdoors only, with ventilation, on a stable surface.
- **Gas or charcoal grills:** use outdoors only, far from the house, deck rails, overhanging branches and windows.
- **Alcohol or solid-fuel stoves:** light and cheap, best for small meals.
- **Rocket stoves and wood fires:** fuel-efficient, use outdoors only and check local fire bans.
- **Solar ovens:** free fuel, slow, need sun.
- **Thermos and insulated cooking:** bring food to a boil, then finish in an insulated container to save fuel.
- **Canned food and ready-to-eat meals:** no cooking needed.

## Safe use
1. Cook outdoors, in a place with plenty of airflow, at least 20 feet from the house if possible and away from combustibles.
2. Keep a fire extinguisher and water nearby.
3. Never leave a flame unattended.
4. Never use a gas oven or range burners to heat your home. Do not use charcoal indoors under any circumstances, even in a fireplace that does not draw well.
5. Store fuel outdoors in approved containers, away from heat.
6. Keep children and pets away.

!! Carbon monoxide has no smell or color. Early symptoms include headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea and confusion. If you suspect it, get to fresh air immediately and call 911. Use battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms.

>> Practice using your stove before an emergency, outdoors, so you know how much fuel a meal uses.

# emergency-meal-planning | Emergency Meal Planning
category: food
priority: normal
tags: meal planning, menu, calories, nutrition, morale, no-cook
sources: usda, ready-gov, cdc
related: calc:food, guide:pantry-staples, guide:cooking-no-power
summary: Plan simple balanced meals from stored food with minimal water and fuel.

## Overview
A plan prevents waste and keeps energy and morale up. Aim for balanced nutrition, familiar flavors and low fuel and water use.

## Steps
1. Calculate daily calorie needs with the Food Calculator.
2. Build a sample 7-day menu from your stored items, then repeat it.
3. Include protein (canned meat or fish, beans, peanut butter), carbohydrates (rice, pasta, crackers, oats), fats (oil, nuts), fruits and vegetables (canned, dried) and dairy or alternatives (shelf-stable milk).
4. Add comfort foods: coffee, tea, chocolate, spices and sauces.
5. Plan the order. Eat perishable food first (fridge, then freezer), then fresh produce, then pantry.
6. Prepare a "no-cook" backup menu for when you cannot use heat.

## Sample no-cook day
- Breakfast: cereal with shelf-stable milk, dried fruit.
- Lunch: tuna or chicken pouch on crackers, canned fruit.
- Dinner: canned beans or chili, tortillas, canned vegetables.
- Snacks: peanut butter, nuts, granola bars.

## Nutrition notes
- Do not ignore protein and fiber.
- Increase calories in cold weather and for physical work.
- Include a multivitamin if the diet is limited, if appropriate for you.
- Drink water regularly.

>> Write the menu and shopping list on paper and keep it in the pantry.

# food-children | Food for Infants and Children in an Emergency
category: food
priority: high
tags: infants, formula, baby food, toddlers, children, breastfeeding
sources: cdc, ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:children-preparedness, guide:water-boiling
summary: Plan formula, safe water, baby food and kid-friendly meals, and protect infants from unsafe preparation.

## Overview
Infants and young children need special attention. They are more vulnerable to dehydration, contamination and inadequate nutrition.

## Infants
- **Breastfeeding** is the safest and most reliable. Keep breastfeeding if you can. Stress can reduce supply but usually continues.
- **Formula:** keep at least a week's supply (more if possible) of the brand your baby uses. Ready-to-feed formula needs no water and is safest if water may be unsafe.
- Mix powdered formula only with safe water. Follow the label exactly. Never dilute or concentrate formula to stretch it.
- Use clean bottles. If you cannot wash them, use disposable bottles or cups.
- Do not give plain water or honey to infants under one year. Do not use homemade formula.
- Keep unopened containers sealed and rotate by date.

## Toddlers and children
- Stock familiar foods: crackers, applesauce, pouches, canned fruit, peanut butter (if no allergies), cereal, shelf-stable milk.
- Offer small, frequent meals and fluids.
- Include comfort foods to reduce stress.
- Check allergies and choking hazards in everything you store.

## Warning signs
Seek medical help right away if an infant or child has signs of dehydration (no tears, dry mouth, fewer wet diapers, sunken eyes or soft spot, unusual sleepiness), vomiting or diarrhea, or fever.

!! Infants should never be given unsafe water. If unsure about the water, use ready-to-feed formula or boiled water that has cooled.

# food-pets | Food and Water for Pets
category: food
priority: normal
tags: pet food, dog, cat, pet water, storage, rotation
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, fema
related: guide:pet-preparedness, checklist:pet-kit, calc:water
summary: Store pet food and water safely and rotate it, including for special diets.

## Overview
Pets depend entirely on you. Plan for their food and water as carefully as for your own.

## What to store
- At least 3 days of food, preferably 2 weeks, in airtight, waterproof containers.
- Cans or pouches with pop-tops, since a can opener may not be available.
- Water: use the water calculator, and add extra for hot weather.
- Medications and any prescription diet, plus enough of the supplements and flea or heartworm prevention they use.
- Bowls and a collapsible bowl for travel.

## Rotation
- Dry pet food: rotate within about 6 months after opening. Check the date on the bag.
- Canned food: rotate by date.
- Replace stored pet water along with your own.

## Special cases
- Cats should not go long without eating. Offer food and water daily even when stressed.
- Birds, small mammals, reptiles and fish have special needs for heat, light and water quality. Plan for power loss and temperature changes.
- Large animals and livestock need feed, water, trailers and an evacuation plan. Contact local agricultural agencies.

!! Chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions, xylitol and many other foods are toxic to pets. Do not feed human leftovers during an emergency.

# special-diets | Special Dietary Requirements
category: food
priority: high
tags: allergies, diabetes, gluten-free, special diet, medical diet, vegetarian, low sodium
sources: cdc, ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:food-storage-basics, guide:medication-storage-continuity, calc:food
summary: Plan food for allergies, diabetes, medical diets, religious or cultural needs and infants.

## Overview
People with medical or dietary needs are at higher risk when food supplies are limited. Plan ahead and discuss with a healthcare provider.

## Common situations
- **Food allergies:** read every label, store allergen-free foods separately, and keep epinephrine autoinjectors with the person, plus a written allergy action plan.
- **Diabetes:** keep insulin, glucose supplies, glucometer and test strips, fast-acting carbohydrates for low blood sugar, and a way to keep insulin cool (follow label guidance). Keep consistent meals and plan carbohydrate choices.
- **Celiac disease or gluten sensitivity:** stock gluten-free staples such as rice, canned vegetables, beans, tuna and gluten-free crackers.
- **Heart disease, kidney disease, high blood pressure:** choose lower-sodium and kidney-appropriate foods per your doctor's guidance.
- **Vegetarian, vegan, halal, kosher:** stock beans, lentils, nuts, grains, shelf-stable plant milk and certified products.
- **Dysphagia or tube feeding:** keep thickeners, supplements and supplies, and power backup for pumps.

## Steps
1. Ask your healthcare provider what to do if your diet is disrupted and what to avoid.
2. Stock safe foods and keep a written list of allergies and dietary rules in your go-bag.
3. Store medications and food-related supplies together and rotate them.
4. Teach family and neighbors about your needs.

!! Never skip or change insulin or other critical medications without medical advice. If blood sugar is dangerously high or low, or you are confused or cannot swallow safely, call 911.
