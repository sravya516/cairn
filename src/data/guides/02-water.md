# water-storage | Emergency Water Storage
category: water
priority: critical
tags: water storage, gallons, drinking water, stored water, supply
sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc, red-cross
related: checklist:water-storage, calc:water, guide:water-storage-containers, guide:safe-water-handling
summary: Store at least 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene, and know where it is.

## Overview
Water is your first priority. People can survive weeks without food but only days without water. Ready.gov and FEMA advise storing at least one gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation, for at least three days and preferably two weeks.

## How much to store
- Plan **1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day** as a minimum. Increase it for hot climates, pregnancy, nursing, illness or exertion.
- Add water for pets (see Pet guide and the water calculator).
- A household of four for 3 days needs about 12 gallons. For 14 days it needs about 56 gallons.
- Water for cooking, extra hygiene or washing needs more. Plan extra, or plan a way to treat more.

## Where to store it
1. Keep water in a cool, dark place off the floor, away from fuel, pesticides and other chemicals.
2. Spread it out: kitchen, garage, closets, car trunk, workplace. Do not put all of it in one spot.
3. Know how to get water from your water heater tank and toilet tank (clean tank only, not bowl) as emergency sources, after shutting off the supply first. Treat it before drinking.
4. Label containers with the fill date and treatment method.

## Storage options
Commercially bottled water in sealed containers is easiest. Use food-grade containers for water you fill yourself (see Water Storage Containers).

## Rotation
- Commercially bottled water: use by the expiration date on the bottle.
- Water you store yourself: commonly recommended to replace about every six months.
- Calendar it with your smoke-alarm battery change.

!! Never use containers that held chemicals, milk or juice (hard to clean). Do not use old bleach jugs or containers that are cracked, leaky or cloudy.

# water-storage-containers | Water Storage Containers
category: water
priority: high
tags: containers, barrels, jugs, food-grade, BPA, storage
sources: ready-gov, cdc, epa
related: guide:water-storage, guide:safe-water-handling, calc:storage
summary: Choose food-grade containers, clean them correctly, and match sizes to what you can carry.

## Overview
The container matters as much as the water. Food-grade plastic or properly cleaned glass or stainless steel keeps water safe. Size affects how easily you can move it.

## Good choices
- **Commercial bottled water:** convenient and sealed, rotate by date.
- **Food-grade water storage jugs and blocks (1–7 gallons):** designed for water, stackable and portable.
- **Food-grade barrels (15–55 gallons):** most efficient for bulk, but heavy once full (a gallon of water weighs about 8.3 lb or 3.8 kg) and need a pump or spigot.
- **Water bladders and bags for tubs:** collect water before an event, but cannot be moved.
- **Clean 2-liter soda bottles with screw caps:** acceptable when thoroughly cleaned and sanitized. Avoid milk and juice jugs.

## Clean and fill your own containers
1. Wash with dish soap and rinse thoroughly.
2. Sanitize with a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented household bleach per quart of water. Swish it around so it touches all surfaces, wait about 2 minutes, then rinse.
3. Fill with safe drinking water (municipal tap water is usually fine; check with your utility if it is private well water).
4. Seal tightly. Do not touch the inside of the cap or neck.
5. Label with the date and store away from sunlight and chemicals.

## Weight and handling
A full 5-gallon container weighs about 42 lb (19 kg). Include smaller containers so children and older adults can carry them.

!! Do not store water in direct sunlight or near gasoline, solvents, or pesticides. Plastic can absorb vapors.

# water-needs-calculation | Household Water Needs
category: water
priority: high
tags: how much water, water calculation, gallons per person, hydration, planning assumptions
sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc
related: calc:water, guide:water-storage, guide:dehydration
summary: Understand the planning assumptions behind water calculations and when to add more.

## Overview
The one-gallon-per-person-per-day rule is a planning figure, not a guarantee. It covers about half a gallon to drink and the rest for minimal hygiene and food preparation. Your real needs depend on climate, health, activity and diet.

## Standard planning assumptions
- Adults and children: 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day. Children's real drinking needs are lower, but sanitation and cooking use the rest.
- Nursing mothers, pregnant women and sick people: need more.
- Hot weather or strenuous activity: can double drinking needs or more.
- Pets: needs vary by size and weather. A common rule of thumb is about 1 ounce per pound of body weight per day.
- Cooking, rehydrating freeze-dried food and washing dishes add extra.

## Steps
1. Count each person and pet in your household.
2. Choose your target (3, 14, or 30 days).
3. Multiply: people × days × 1 gallon, plus pets and extras.
4. Subtract nothing for the water heater or toilet tank. Treat those as backup only.
5. Decide how you will treat more water if the supply runs out.

## Example
Two adults, one child, one 30-lb dog for 3 days: (3 people × 3 days × 1 gal) + about 0.25 gal per day for the dog × 3 = about 9.75 gallons, so store about 10 gallons plus extra.

# water-treatment-overview | Filtration, Purification and Disinfection: What's the Difference?
category: water
priority: critical
tags: filtration, purification, disinfection, water treatment, boil, bleach, filter
sources: cdc, epa, ready-gov
related: guide:water-boiling, guide:water-disinfection, guide:water-filters, guide:unsafe-water-sources
summary: Filtering, disinfecting and purifying are different. Know what each removes before you trust a method.

## Overview
People often use these words as if they mean the same thing. They do not. Using the wrong method can leave water unsafe.

## The three terms
- **Filtration** removes particles and, depending on the filter, some microorganisms (bacteria, protozoa such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium). Many portable filters do not remove viruses or dissolved chemicals.
- **Disinfection** kills or inactivates microorganisms using heat (boiling) or chemicals (chlorine, chlorine dioxide, iodine). It does not remove dissolved chemicals or heavy metals.
- **Purification** means making water safe to drink by removing or inactivating all biological threats, often combining filtration and disinfection. Distillation can also remove many chemicals.

## What each method handles
- **Boiling:** kills bacteria, viruses and protozoa. Does not remove chemicals, fuel, heavy metals or nitrates.
- **Chemical disinfection:** works on bacteria, viruses and (depending on product and time) many protozoa. Cryptosporidium is resistant to chlorine, so filtering first helps.
- **Portable filters:** read the label. Look for the size of pore and tested claims for bacteria, protozoa and viruses. Chemicals usually need activated carbon or other media, and many chemicals cannot be removed at home.

## A good routine for questionable water
1. Let sediment settle and pour through a clean cloth or coffee filter.
2. Filter with a rated filter if available.
3. Disinfect by boiling or by chemical treatment.
4. Store treated water in clean, covered containers.

!! No home method reliably removes chemical contamination such as fuel, pesticides or industrial chemicals. If water smells of chemicals, looks oily or comes from a contaminated area, do not drink it. Use another source.

# water-boiling | Boiling Water
category: water
priority: critical
tags: boil water, boiling, rolling boil, boil advisory, high altitude
sources: cdc, epa, ready-gov
related: guide:water-treatment-overview, guide:unsafe-water-sources
summary: Boiling is the most reliable way to make microbiologically contaminated water safe to drink.

## Overview
Boiling kills disease-causing bacteria, viruses and protozoa. It is the standard recommendation during a boil-water advisory.

## Steps
1. If the water is cloudy, let it settle and pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter into a pot.
2. Bring the water to a **rolling boil** (large, continuous bubbles).
3. Keep it at a rolling boil for **1 minute**. At elevations above about 6,500 feet (2,000 m), boil for **3 minutes**.
4. Let it cool naturally. Do not add ice made from untreated water.
5. Store in clean, covered containers.
6. If the water tastes flat, pour it back and forth between clean containers or add a tiny pinch of salt per quart.

## Uses during a boil advisory
Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, preparing food and beverages, brushing teeth, washing fruits and vegetables, making ice, and preparing baby formula. Dishes can be washed in hot soapy water and rinsed or sanitized with a bleach solution. Bathing is generally safe if you avoid swallowing the water, but check the advisory.

## When boiling is not enough
Boiling does not remove chemicals, fuel, heavy metals or nitrates. In fact, boiling can concentrate some contaminants as water evaporates. Do not rely on boiling if chemical contamination is suspected.

!! Take care with hot water. Scalds are common in disasters. Never boil water in an unventilated space on fuel-burning stoves.

# water-disinfection | Disinfecting Water with Bleach, Tablets and Other Chemicals
category: water
priority: critical
tags: bleach, chlorine, disinfect, purification tablets, drops per gallon, iodine
sources: cdc, epa, red-cross
related: guide:water-treatment-overview, guide:water-boiling, guide:water-filters
summary: Use plain unscented household bleach or purpose-made tablets correctly, with correct amounts and contact time.

## Overview
When you cannot boil water, chemical disinfection is an alternative. It kills most bacteria and viruses but does not remove chemicals and is less reliable against some protozoa.

## Using household bleach
1. Check the label. Use **regular, unscented** household bleach with **sodium hypochlorite** as the only active ingredient. Do not use scented, color-safe, splash-less, or bleach with added cleaners.
2. Note the percent: most regular bleach is **6%** or **8.25%** sodium hypochlorite.
3. If the water is cloudy, strain it through a clean cloth or coffee filter first.
4. Per **1 gallon** of water, add about **8 drops (about 1/8 teaspoon) of 6% bleach**, or about **6 drops of 8.25% bleach**. Double the amount if the water is very cloudy, colored or very cold.
5. Stir or shake and wait **at least 30 minutes**.
6. The water should have a slight chlorine smell. If not, repeat once and wait another 15 minutes. If it still lacks a chlorine smell, discard it.

>> Verify these drop counts against the current CDC or EPA table whenever you are online. They depend on the bleach strength printed on your bottle.

## Using purification tablets or drops
Follow the manufacturer's label exactly for dose, contact time and water temperature. Chlorine dioxide tablets are effective against many protozoa, but only with the stated contact time. Iodine is not recommended for pregnant people or people with thyroid conditions, and for long-term use.

## Limits
- Does not remove chemicals, metals or radioactive material.
- Cryptosporidium resists chlorine. Filter first if you suspect it, or boil instead.
- Bleach loses strength over time. Replace the bottle about every year and store away from heat.

!! Never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar or other cleaners. It produces toxic gases. Keep bleach away from children.

# water-filters | Water Filters and Portable Filters
category: water
priority: high
tags: water filter, portable filter, pump filter, gravity filter, pore size, carbon
sources: cdc, epa, ready-gov
related: guide:water-treatment-overview, guide:water-disinfection, guide:unsafe-water-sources
summary: Understand what a filter does and does not remove, and how to choose and maintain one.

## Overview
Filters physically strain out particles and microorganisms. The pore size and tested claims tell you what they remove. They are not all alike.

## Types
- **Pump and squeeze filters:** compact; usually remove bacteria and protozoa. Many do not remove viruses.
- **Gravity filters:** easy for groups. Same limits as above unless labeled otherwise.
- **Straw filters:** personal emergency use.
- **Ceramic and countertop systems:** good for home use, need cleaning.
- **Activated carbon:** improves taste and can reduce some chemicals, but is not a complete treatment for microbes.
- **Reverse osmosis and distillation:** can remove many chemicals but need power, parts or fuel and produce waste water.
- **Purifier devices:** include a virus barrier (chemical or pore size) and are tested to purification standards. Read the label.

## Choosing
1. Look for third-party testing claims (for example NSF/ANSI standards) and the stated pore size (for example 0.2 micron removes bacteria and protozoa).
2. Check whether it claims removal of viruses. If not, add chemical disinfection or boiling.
3. Keep spare cartridges.
4. Learn the capacity. Clogged filters slow down, and overused ones fail.

## Use and care
- Pre-filter cloudy water.
- Follow cleaning and backflush instructions.
- Never let a ceramic or hollow-fiber filter freeze, which can crack it invisibly.
- Keep clean and dirty hands and containers separate.

!! If you do not know what a filter removes, treat the filtered water by boiling or disinfection too.

# unsafe-water-sources | Identifying Unsafe or Contaminated Water
category: water
priority: critical
tags: contaminated water, flood water, chemical contamination, unsafe, sewage, boil advisory
sources: cdc, epa, fema
related: guide:water-treatment-overview, guide:water-boiling, guide:flooding
summary: Know the signs and situations in which water should not be used or cannot be made safe at home.

## Overview
Not all water can be made safe. Biological hazards can often be treated. Chemical and radiological hazards usually cannot.

## Treat or avoid
- **Do not drink** floodwater, water from storm drains, or water that mixes with sewage.
- **Do not drink** water that smells of fuel, chemicals or sewage, looks oily, or has a strange color after a flood or spill.
- **Do not trust** a household well flooded by storm surge until it has been disinfected and tested.
- Boil-water, do-not-drink and do-not-use advisories mean exactly what they say. Obey official notices.

## Safer sources in emergencies
- Stored water and sealed bottled water.
- Melted ice cubes (not ice from a flooded freezer).
- Water from your water heater tank or ice from your freezer (clean).
- Water from clean toilet tanks (not bowls), if no cleaners were added.
- Rainwater collected from a clean surface.
- Treat natural sources (streams, ponds) only if nothing better exists, and filter and disinfect them.

## Signs and cautions
- Dead fish, algae blooms or foam in a water body.
- Nearby industrial, agricultural or mine sites.
- Water that smells like gasoline, solvents or sewage.
- Illness among people or pets using the water.

## If you drank unsafe water
Watch for vomiting, diarrhea, fever and cramps. Rest, drink safe fluids and seek medical care if symptoms are severe, last more than a day or two, or affect infants, older adults or people with weak immune systems. Call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222 in the U.S.) for suspected chemical exposure.

!! When you cannot confirm the water is safe, do not drink it. Use another source and report suspected contamination to local authorities.

# water-collection | Emergency Water Collection and Rainwater
category: water
priority: normal
tags: rainwater, collection, water heater, finding water, tarp
sources: cdc, epa, ready-gov
related: guide:water-treatment-overview, guide:unsafe-water-sources, guide:safe-water-handling
summary: Where you can find water in an emergency and how to collect rain safely, subject to local rules.

## Overview
If your supply runs out, look for safe sources, collect what you can and always treat water you did not store yourself.

## Before the emergency
- Fill bathtubs, sinks and spare containers when a storm or outage is forecast. Sanitize tubs first and use covered containers for drinking water.
- Fill every clean container, and freeze water in plastic bottles to help keep a fridge or cooler cold.

## Water already in your home
- Water heater tank: turn off power or gas first and let it cool, close the water inlet valve, open the drain at the bottom. Strain and treat it.
- Ice in the freezer.
- Toilet tanks (not bowls) if no cleaner or tank tablets were used. Treat before drinking.
- Canned vegetables' liquid and other beverages.

## Collecting rain
1. Use clean tarps, plastic sheeting or clean gutters and containers. Do not use roofs coated with chemical treatments or in areas of heavy ash or smoke.
2. Let the first rain wash the surface for several minutes before collecting.
3. Collect into clean, covered, food-grade containers.
4. Filter and disinfect before drinking.

## Other considerations
- Some places restrict rainwater collection. Check local rules.
- Avoid rain that falls through wildfire ash, chemical smoke or industrial fallout.
- Natural sources (rivers, lakes, springs) should be a last resort and must be filtered and disinfected.

!! Rainwater collected from roofs can carry bird droppings, metals and chemicals. Always treat it.

# safe-water-handling | Safe Water Handling and Rotation
category: water
priority: high
tags: water handling, rotation, shelf life, hygiene, storage maintenance
sources: cdc, epa, red-cross
related: guide:water-storage, guide:water-storage-containers, guide:water-treatment-overview
summary: Keep stored water clean and keep it fresh with simple habits and a rotation schedule.

## Overview
Safe water can become unsafe if handled poorly. Clean hands, clean containers and a regular schedule keep it safe.

## Handling habits
1. Wash hands with soap and safe water before handling drinking water.
2. Use a clean cup or ladle or a tap. Never dip hands, or reuse a cup that touched mouths.
3. Keep containers covered, preferably with the original lid.
4. Keep drinking water separate from water used for cleaning or washing.
5. Label treated water and untreated water clearly, and use different containers.
6. Wash and sanitize containers between uses.

## Shelf life and rotation
- Commercially bottled water: use by the printed expiration date.
- Home-filled water: commonly recommended to replace about every six months, whether tap water treated by a utility or treated well water.
- If water tastes or smells off, discard it. Flat taste after storage is normal and harmless; you can pour it between containers to aerate it.
- Use older water for plants or cleaning and refill containers with fresh water.

## Maintenance schedule
1. Add "replace water" to your calendar every six months.
2. Inspect containers for leaks, cracks, algae or cloudiness.
3. Keep containers off concrete floors if possible and away from heat and light.
4. Record the date and any treatment on each container.

>> Pair water rotation with daylight-saving time changes, so you only have to remember one routine.
