summary: Smoke can travel hundreds of miles. Check the air quality, stay indoors with clean air, and use a well-fitting N95 respirator if you must go out.

## Overview
Wildfire smoke contains tiny particles that can enter deep into the lungs and bloodstream. It can irritate eyes, throat and lungs and make heart and lung conditions worse, even far from the fire. Smoke can arrive with little warning and last days or weeks.

## Know the air quality
The U.S. Air Quality Index (AQI) uses color categories. Check your local reading on AirNow or a local alert.
- **Green (0–50) Good.** **Yellow (51–100) Moderate:** very sensitive people may notice effects.
- **Orange (101–150) Unhealthy for sensitive groups:** children, older adults, pregnant people and people with heart or lung disease should limit time outdoors.
- **Red (151–200) Unhealthy:** everyone should reduce outdoor activity.
- **Purple (201–300) Very unhealthy** and **Maroon (301+) Hazardous:** stay indoors with clean air and avoid exertion.
If you cannot see far or smell smoke, treat the air as unhealthy even without a reading.

## Protect your home
1. Keep windows and doors closed. Do not use swamp coolers or whole-house fans that pull in outside air.
2. Run air conditioning on **recirculate** with a clean, high-efficiency filter (rated MERV 13 or higher if your system allows it). Replace filters sooner in smoky weather.
3. Create a **clean-air room**: choose one room with few leaks, close the door and run a portable **HEPA air cleaner** in it.
4. A do-it-yourself filter can help when you cannot buy a purifier: attach a MERV 13 furnace filter to a modern box fan, following published designs. Never leave it unattended, and use a fan in good condition.
5. Avoid things that add indoor pollution: burning candles, gas stoves and fireplaces, frying food and vacuuming without a HEPA filter. Do not use ozone generators.

## If you must go out
- Wear a properly fitted **N95 or equivalent respirator**. Cloth masks and surgical masks do not filter smoke particles well. A respirator works only if it seals to the face.
- Limit exertion. Heavy breathing pulls more smoke into your lungs.
- In the car, close windows and vents and set the air to recirculate.

## When to get help
Seek care for chest pain, trouble breathing, wheezing that does not ease, severe cough, dizziness or fainting. People with asthma should follow their action plan and keep rescue medication close (see Asthma Attacks). Call 911 for severe symptoms.

## Pets and animals
Keep them indoors. Watch for coughing, eye irritation, lethargy or breathing problems.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming a mask from the drugstore protects you.
- Opening windows to "air out" the house while smoke persists.
- Exercising outdoors when the air is orange or worse.

!! Facial hair and loose fit break the seal, and a respirator that does not seal protects very little. Respirators can also make breathing harder for people with lung or heart disease, so ask your clinician before relying on one.

sources: uscg, nws, red-cross
related: guide:drowning-water-rescue, guide:hypothermia, guide:tsunamis, guide:ventilation-co-safety, guide:fire-prevention-escape
summary: Wear a life jacket, tell someone your plan, and know how to call for help and what to do in cold water, a capsize or a person overboard.

## Overview
Most boating deaths involve drowning, and most victims were not wearing a life jacket. Weather, cold water, fire, running aground and carbon monoxide are other major risks. A few simple habits and a plan make a large difference.

## Before you go
1. **Wear a life jacket** that fits, and make sure everyone aboard does. Children must wear one at all times near the water.
2. File a **float plan** with someone ashore: where you are going, who is aboard and when you will return.
3. Check the weather and marine forecast, including wind, waves, thunderstorms and tides. Do not go if a storm or small-craft advisory is likely.
4. Carry a working marine VHF radio, a charged phone in a waterproof case, flares or a signal device, a whistle, a first-aid kit, a fire extinguisher and a bailer or pump. A personal locator beacon or EPIRB can send your location when you cannot call.
5. Do not drink alcohol while operating a boat.

## Calling for help
- **Marine VHF channel 16** is the international distress and calling channel. For life-threatening danger say **"Mayday, Mayday, Mayday,"** then your boat's name, position, nature of the emergency and number of people aboard.
- If you have no radio and have cell service, call 911 or the Coast Guard.
- Use flares, a mirror or a bright flag to signal. Stay together.

## Capsized or swamped
1. **Stay with the boat.** It floats, is easier to see and keeps you out of the water. Climb onto the hull if you can.
2. Put on life jackets and keep everyone together. Do not try to swim for shore unless it is very close and you are a strong swimmer.

## Person overboard
Shout "Man overboard!", point at the person continuously, throw anything that floats, slow the boat and turn back carefully. **Do not jump in.** Reach or throw. After recovery, treat for hypothermia and watch for breathing problems.

## Cold water
Cold water can cause gasping and panic within the first minute, loss of strength in about ten minutes and hypothermia over the next hour or more. Control your breathing first. Float with your life jacket on, keep your head out of the water, and curl up to save heat if alone. See the Hypothermia guide.

## Fire, fuel and carbon monoxide
- If you smell fuel, ventilate and do not start the engine.
- For a fire: turn off the engine and fuel, keep people upwind and use an extinguisher.
- Carbon monoxide builds up near engines and generators, even outdoors on a boat. Keep passengers away from the stern when the engine runs, and install a marine CO alarm.

!! Do not go back into the water to look for gear. Weather changes quickly. If a tsunami warning is issued, follow harbor and Coast Guard instructions.

# traveling-abroad-emergencies | Emergencies While Traveling Abroad
category: evacuation
priority: normal

sources: state, cdc, ready-gov
related: guide:emergency-documents, guide:contact-planning, guide:medication-storage-continuity, guide:go-bag, guide:family-comms-plan
summary: Learn the local emergency number before you go, carry copies of documents and medicines, and know how to reach your embassy.

## Overview
This app focuses on the United States, but emergencies happen overseas too. Emergency numbers, languages, ambulance and police practices, and health systems differ. A little planning before you leave makes a large difference.

## Before you go
1. **Look up the emergency number** for each country on your route and save it in your phone and on paper. Do not assume it is 911.
2. If you are a U.S. citizen, consider enrolling in the State Department's **Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP)** to receive safety alerts and be easier to reach in a crisis. Check travel advisories for your destination.
3. Buy **travel health insurance** that covers emergency care abroad and **medical evacuation**. Domestic insurance and Medicare often do not.
4. See a clinician well before the trip about vaccinations and medications. Carry medicines in original containers with a copy of the prescriptions. Check local rules, since some common medicines are restricted in some countries.
5. Keep **copies of your passport**, visas, insurance and itinerary on paper and stored securely online, separate from the originals. Leave a copy and your plan with someone at home.
6. Download offline maps, a translation tool and your key documents for use without service. See the offline maps guide.
7. Agree on a family check-in routine.

## Common emergency numbers
These are widely used. Numbers change, so **confirm them before you travel**.
- **112:** the emergency number in the European Union and in many other countries, and often works on mobile phones in many places.
- **911:** United States, Canada and Mexico.
- **999:** United Kingdom. **000:** Australia. **111:** New Zealand.
- **110 (police)** and **119 (fire and ambulance):** Japan.
In a few places, police, fire and ambulance have separate numbers. If a call will not connect, try 112.

## If something happens
- **Medical emergency:** call the local number, say your location first and ask for an English speaker if needed. Contact your insurer's assistance line early.
- **Lost or stolen passport, arrest or serious injury:** contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate. U.S. citizens can reach the State Department's Overseas Citizens Services by phone. The numbers are on travel.state.gov, so save them before you go.
- **Disaster or unrest:** follow local authorities, contact family, check embassy alerts and move to a safe place. Know your accommodation's exits and plan.
- **Theft:** report to local police and your embassy if documents are lost. Cancel cards.

## Common mistakes
- Not carrying enough medication for delays.
- Relying on a single phone and one payment card.
- Ignoring local advice and travel warnings.

!! Laws and customs differ. Carry ID, respect local rules and avoid risky areas. If you are not a U.S. citizen, contact your own country's embassy.

related: guide:electrical-safety, guide:lightning, guide:cpr-aed, guide:burns, guide:shock
summary: Make sure the power is off before touching a shocked person. Call 911, start CPR if needed and treat burns. A lightning victim carries no charge.

## Electric shock
Electricity can stop the heart, cause burns and injure nerves and organs, even when the skin looks fine.
1. **Do not touch the person** while they may still be in contact with the source. You could be shocked too.
2. **Turn off the power** at the switch, breaker or plug if you can do so safely. If you cannot, use a dry, non-conducting object such as dry wood, a plastic broom handle or a rope to push the source away. Do not use anything wet or metal.
3. **Call 911.**
4. **Downed power lines:** stay at least 35 feet (10 meters) away. Assume they are live. Leave the area and wait for the utility. If a line falls on your car, stay inside and call for help. If you must leave because of fire, jump clear with feet together and shuffle away.
5. Once the person is safe to touch, check responsiveness and breathing. **If not breathing normally, begin CPR and use an AED.** Electric shock can cause a sudden irregular heartbeat.
6. Treat burns: cool them with cool running water and cover loosely with a clean dry dressing (see Burns). Entry and exit burns can be small but the injury underneath can be deep.
7. Lay the person down, keep them warm and watch for shock.

## Lightning strike
- **Lightning victims do not carry an electrical charge.** It is safe to touch them.
- Move the victim to a safe place if the storm continues, but do not put yourself at risk.
- Call 911. Check breathing and start CPR and use an AED if needed.
- **If several people are struck, help those who appear lifeless first.** Lightning often stops the heart and breathing, and quick CPR can save them. People who are conscious and moving have a better outlook.
- Look for burns, hearing and vision problems, broken bones and confusion.

## Medical care afterward
Every person who received a significant electric shock or a lightning strike should be evaluated by a medical professional, even if they feel fine. Problems with heart rhythm, muscles and kidneys can appear later.

## Prevention
Keep away from water and electricity, use GFCI outlets near water, keep ladders away from power lines, never touch fallen wires and follow the lightning rule: when thunder roars, go indoors.

!! Never touch a person who may still be connected to a live wire or a high-voltage source. Wait for the utility or rescuers.
