# gardening-food-security | Gardening for Food Security
category: selfreliance
priority: normal

sources: usda, epa

summary: A garden adds fresh food and resilience, but it takes seasons to build. Start small and grow what you eat.

## Overview
Gardens do not replace stored food quickly, but they improve food variety, lower costs and build skills. Start before a crisis.

## Getting started
1. Choose a sunny spot (6–8 hours of sun) with good drainage and easy access to water.
2. Test your soil for lead and nutrients, particularly in urban or older areas. Use raised beds with clean soil if contamination is a concern, and consult local extension services.
3. Start with easy crops that suit your climate: tomatoes, beans, squash, greens, carrots, potatoes, herbs.
4. Add calorie-dense crops such as potatoes, winter squash, beans, corn and sweet potatoes.
5. Plant perennials: fruit trees, berries, asparagus, rhubarb.
6. Compost kitchen and yard scraps to improve soil.
7. Plan for water: drip irrigation, mulch and rain barrels, if allowed.
8. Keep records: planting dates, varieties and results.

## Garden safety
- Wash produce. Do not use unsafe water for irrigation of crops eaten raw.
- Use gloves and wash hands. Update your tetanus shot.
- Avoid using treated lumber or contaminated materials for raised beds.

## Realistic expectations
A small garden may supply fresh produce for part of the year. Producing most of a household's calories requires significant land, time and experience.

>> Local cooperative extension offices provide free, region-specific gardening advice.

# seed-storage | Seed Storage
category: selfreliance
priority: normal
tags: seeds, seed saving, germination, storage, heirloom, viability
sources: usda
related: guide:gardening-food-security
summary: Store seeds cool, dry and dark, and check viability so they grow when you need them.

## Overview
Seeds are small, light and valuable. Stored properly, many remain viable for years.

## Storage rules
1. Keep seeds **cool, dry and dark**. Heat and moisture are the enemies.
2. Use airtight containers such as glass jars or foil packets, with a desiccant packet (silica gel) to absorb moisture.
3. Label with the variety and date.
4. Store in a cool place, such as a refrigerator or cool interior closet. Freezing can work for well-dried seed, but moisture damages seeds, so keep them completely dry and let containers warm before opening.
5. Do not store in a hot garage or attic.

## Shelf life (general guidelines)
- Onions, leeks, parsnips: 1–2 years.
- Carrots, peppers, beans, peas, spinach, sweet corn: about 2–3 years.
- Tomatoes, squash, lettuce, cucumbers, beets, brassicas: 3–5 years or more.
These vary by conditions and the seed's quality.

## Test viability
Place 10 seeds on a damp paper towel in a bag, keep warm, and count how many sprout in a week or two. If fewer than 70–80% germinate, plant more densely or replace the seeds.

## Saving your own
Choose open-pollinated or heirloom varieties, since hybrids do not breed true. Dry seeds thoroughly before storing.

>> Buy seeds appropriate to your climate and growing season, and rotate stock by planting.

# preservation-canning-safety | Food Preservation, Canning and Dehydration
category: selfreliance
priority: high
tags: canning, preserving, dehydrating, botulism, pressure canner, water bath, drying
sources: usda, cdc
related: guide:food-rotation, guide:gardening-food-security, guide:pantry-staples
summary: Preserve food safely using tested methods. Home canning errors can cause botulism, a deadly illness.

## Overview
Preserving extends food supplies through drying, freezing, fermenting and canning. Safety depends on following tested, up-to-date procedures exactly.

## Canning safety
1. Use **tested recipes** from reliable sources such as the USDA's Complete Guide to Home Canning or the National Center for Home Food Preservation.
2. **High-acid foods** (most fruits, pickles, jams) can be processed in a boiling-water canner.
3. **Low-acid foods** (vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, soups) must be processed in a **pressure canner**. A boiling-water bath is not safe because it does not reach the temperature needed to destroy botulism spores.
4. Use proper jars, new lids and a calibrated pressure gauge. Adjust times and pressure for altitude.
5. Follow processing times exactly. Do not invent recipes or change ingredients, jar sizes or thickness.
6. Check seals. Store jars without rings in a cool, dark place. Label with dates. Use within a year or so for best quality.
7. Do not taste questionable food. Discard any jar with a bulging lid, leaks, spurting liquid, off odor or mold. Dispose of it safely.
8. Boiling home-canned low-acid food for 10 minutes before eating (more at higher altitude) is a recommended precaution in some guidance. Follow current USDA advice.

## Dehydration and drying
- Dry foods thoroughly to a safe moisture level using a dehydrator, oven or sun, per tested instructions.
- Store dried foods in airtight containers, in a cool, dark place. Check for moisture and mold.
- Rehydrate with safe water and cook meat thoroughly.

## Freezing and fermenting
Freeze at 0°F (-18°C). Fermenting vegetables needs the right salt ratio and clean equipment. Use tested recipes.

!! Botulism is rare but life-threatening. Symptoms include double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech and muscle weakness. Call 911 right away.

# home-maintenance-resources | Home Maintenance, Waste and Household Resource Management
category: selfreliance
priority: normal

related: guide:emergency-toilets-waste, guide:cleaning-sanitation, guide:extended-disruptions
summary: Keep your home safe and working with basic repairs, tools, spare parts and careful use of supplies.

## Overview
During long disruptions, you may not be able to call a repairer. Basic skills and supplies keep your home safe and reduce risks.

## Basic skills and tools
- Know how to shut off water, gas and electricity, and to relight pilot lights only if the manufacturer's instructions allow.
- Keep a basic toolkit: hammer, screwdrivers, wrenches, pliers, utility knife, saw, tape measure, duct tape, rope, work gloves, safety glasses.
- Keep spare parts: fuses, faucet washers, hose clamps, filters, light bulbs, smoke alarm batteries.
- Keep tarps, plastic sheeting, plywood, nails and screws for temporary repairs.
- Learn to patch a roof or window temporarily, or fix a leak.
- Learn to use a fire extinguisher.

## Maintenance routines
- Service heating, cooling and chimneys yearly.
- Clean gutters, check roofs and trim trees.
- Test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms monthly.
- Maintain generators, vehicles and tools.

## Waste
Plan for trash, human waste and wastewater when services stop (see Emergency Toilets). Compost organic waste if possible, store trash in sealed bins, and avoid dumping chemicals.

## Resource management
1. Keep a written inventory of food, water, fuel and batteries.
2. Ration sensibly: use perishable items first, and track consumption to project how long supplies last.
3. Share resources with neighbors where possible.

>> A written inventory with dates is the quickest way to know what is running low.

# emergency-budgeting | Emergency Budgeting and Financial Preparedness
category: selfreliance
priority: high
tags: emergency fund, budget, cash, insurance, financial preparedness, costs
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: calc:budget, guide:emergency-documents, guide:prepare-30-days
summary: Build preparedness gradually with a budget, an emergency fund and the right insurance and records.

## Overview
Preparedness does not require spending thousands at once. A steady plan spreads costs and gives you financial resilience.

## Steps
1. Decide your goal level (72 hours, two weeks, 30 days) and use the Budget Calculator to estimate costs.
2. Start with the basics: water, food, light, first aid and communications.
3. Set a small monthly amount or buy two extra items each shopping trip.
4. Use sales and bulk buying for shelf-stable items you eat anyway.
5. Build an emergency fund: aim for several months of expenses over time, starting with a small cushion such as a few hundred dollars.
6. Keep some **cash in small bills** at home, since ATMs and card readers may be down.
7. Review insurance: homeowners or renters, flood, auto, health, life and disability. Understand deductibles and exclusions, and waiting periods for flood policies.
8. Keep copies of financial records, policy numbers and contact numbers for banks and insurers.
9. Photograph or list your belongings for claims.

## Priorities when money is tight
Water first, then medications, then food, then light and communications, then comfort and extras.

## Avoid
- Panic buying and hoarding at inflated prices.
- Buying equipment you do not know how to use.
- Scams after disasters.

>> Preparedness is a habit more than a purchase.

# community-resilience | Community Resilience
category: selfreliance
priority: normal
tags: community, resilience, mutual aid, volunteering, CERT, skills sharing
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: guide:neighborhood-preparedness, guide:neighborhood-coordination
summary: Communities that know and help each other recover faster. Build skills, networks and shared resources.

## Overview
Strong social networks are among the best predictors of recovery after disasters. You and your neighbors are the first responders.

## Ways to build resilience
1. Join or start a neighborhood preparedness group, faith community effort or local volunteer organization.
2. Train: CERT, first aid and CPR, Stop the Bleed, amateur radio, shelter management.
3. Volunteer with your local Red Cross chapter, fire department auxiliary or emergency management.
4. Share information: map local hazards, resources and vulnerable residents.
5. Build shared resources, such as a tool library, community garden, communications plan or a generator or water for a neighborhood hub.
6. Support local businesses and food systems to keep them resilient.
7. Engage with local planning, such as hazard mitigation and evacuation plans.
8. Include everyone: older adults, people with disabilities, people who speak other languages, renters and people who are isolated.

## During recovery
Check on each other, share accurate information and coordinate with official efforts. Support people with long-term needs, because recovery can take months or years.

>> Introduce yourself to three neighbors this month. That one step is the foundation of everything else.

# extended-disruptions | Extended Infrastructure Disruptions
category: selfreliance
priority: normal

sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc
related: guide:prepare-30-days, guide:extended-emergency-planning, guide:community-resilience
summary: Plan for weeks without normal power, water, fuel, transport or supply chains, and what to do first.

## Overview
Rare but severe events (major hurricanes, earthquakes, cyber or physical attacks on infrastructure, pandemics) can disrupt systems for weeks. Households that plan well fare better, and help their neighbors.

## What can fail
- **Electricity:** refrigeration, heating and cooling, water pumping, communications and fuel pumps.
- **Water and sewer:** pressure loss, contamination, and no flushing.
- **Fuel and transport:** shortages, closed roads and limited deliveries.
- **Communications:** cell and internet outages.
- **Supply chains:** grocery shelves empty within days. Pharmacies close.
- **Services:** banks, ATMs, payment systems and healthcare.

## First 72 hours
Stay informed, conserve resources, check on neighbors and follow official guidance. Use perishable food first. Protect water. Avoid unnecessary travel.

## Weeks 1–4
1. Ration water and treat any extra sources you use.
2. Set up safe sanitation, cooking, lighting and heating routines.
3. Maintain hygiene to avoid illness.
4. Coordinate with neighbors for shared resources, security and skills.
5. Keep morale up with routine, rest and meaningful activity.
6. Maintain medications and health as best you can, and contact clinicians where possible.
7. Plan to relocate if your home becomes unsafe, with fuel and documents ready.

## Mental resilience
Stress, loss and uncertainty are normal. Keep a schedule, assign tasks, get exercise and sleep, and talk with others. Seek help if distress is severe.

!! Do not use unsafe methods to heat, cook or provide power. Do not stockpile fuel or chemicals beyond what is safe and legal.
