# hurricanes | Hurricanes
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: hurricane, storm surge, tropical storm, evacuation zone, wind, coastal
sources: noaa, nws, fema, ready-gov
related: checklist:hurricane, guide:flooding, guide:evacuation-planning, guide:generator-safety, guide:extended-power-outages
summary: Hurricanes bring storm surge, wind, rain and tornadoes. Know your evacuation zone and leave when ordered.

## Understanding the threat
Hurricanes are large rotating storms that form over warm ocean water. Their hazards include **storm surge** (often the deadliest, a rise of seawater onto land), flooding from heavy rain far inland, destructive wind and tornadoes. The Saffir-Simpson scale (Category 1–5) rates wind only; it does not measure surge or rain. Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Learn your evacuation zone and route. Zones are based on surge risk, not just wind.
2. Review insurance. Flood damage is usually not covered by homeowners policies, and flood policies often have a waiting period.
3. Protect your home: trim trees, secure outdoor items, install shutters or have plywood ready, and service the sump pump and gutters.
4. Fuel your vehicle and keep it at least half full in storm season.
5. Prepare a go-bag and keep documents and medications ready.
6. Plan for pets and people with special needs.

## Supplies required
Water and food for at least 3 days (preferably more), battery or hand-crank radio, flashlights, first-aid kit, medications, power banks, cash, tools, tarps and a generator plan used outdoors only.

## Warning signs
Watches mean hurricane conditions are possible within 48 hours. Warnings mean they are expected within 36 hours. Listen to NOAA Weather Radio and local officials. Evacuation orders may come before landfall.

## During the event
1. Stay indoors, away from windows, in an interior room on the lowest safe floor not subject to flooding. Do not go outside during the calm "eye".
2. Keep your radio on for updates.
3. If water enters, move to higher floors. Do not retreat to a closed attic without an exit tool.
4. Do not use a generator, grill or camp stove indoors.

## Evacuation considerations
Leave early if ordered, if you live in a surge zone, a mobile home, or if you have medical needs. Leave before the winds arrive, and follow designated routes. Expect traffic and fuel shortages. Know where you will stay.

## Shelter considerations
Public shelters are a last resort and do not always accept pets, so check local arrangements in advance. Staying with friends or family outside the zone is often better. Bring your own supplies.

## Afterward
1. Wait for officials to say it is safe. Avoid floodwater, which can hide debris and electrical hazards and can be contaminated.
2. Stay away from downed power lines and report them.
3. Use caution with chainsaws, generators and cleanup. Wear protective gear. See the Flooding guide for cleanup.
4. Photograph damage for insurance and contact your insurer.
5. Check on neighbors and boil water if advised.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting to see how bad it gets before leaving.
- Driving through floodwater.
- Using generators or grills indoors or in garages.
- Leaving only after services fail.
- Relying on the category number, not surge and rain forecasts.

## Special considerations for families
Pack for children (food, formula, comfort items, diapers, games). Have a school or childcare reunification plan. Keep pets with you and carry their documents. Plan for older relatives and people with medical equipment before the storm.

# tornadoes | Tornadoes
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: tornado, twister, funnel cloud, shelter, severe weather, safe room
sources: nws, noaa, fema, ready-gov
related: checklist:tornado, guide:severe-thunderstorms, guide:shelter-in-place-planning
summary: Move to the lowest interior room of a sturdy building at the first warning. Mobile homes and vehicles are not safe.

## Understanding the threat
Tornadoes are violently rotating columns of air that can destroy buildings, throw debris and appear with little warning. They form most often in severe thunderstorms and can occur at night and in any state. They are rated on the Enhanced Fujita scale (EF0–EF5).

## Preparing beforehand
1. Choose your shelter: a basement, storm cellar, or a small interior room (bathroom, closet, hallway) on the lowest floor, away from windows. A FEMA-style safe room or storm shelter is best.
2. Practice with the family and pets.
3. Keep helmets, sturdy shoes, a flashlight, whistle, phone and a battery radio in the shelter.
4. Sign up for weather alerts and keep a NOAA Weather Radio with an alert function, especially to wake you at night.
5. Know the difference between a watch and a warning.

## Supplies required
Helmets (bike or sports), sturdy shoes, flashlight, whistle, first-aid kit, water, medications, phone chargers, blankets or mattresses to cover yourself.

## Warning signs
A **tornado watch** means conditions are favorable. A **tornado warning** means a tornado has been seen or indicated by radar: take shelter now. Signs include a dark green or greenish sky, a wall cloud, large hail, a loud roar like a freight train, and debris in the air.

## During the event
1. Go to your shelter immediately. Get low, face down, and cover your head and neck with your arms, a mattress, or heavy blankets.
2. Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls. Do not open windows.
3. If you are in a mobile or manufactured home, leave and go to a sturdy building before the storm if possible.
4. If caught in a vehicle, do not try to outrun it in traffic. Drive to a nearby sturdy building if you can safely reach one. If not, park, keep your seat belt on, put your head below the windows and cover it. Avoid overpasses.
5. If caught outside with no shelter, find the lowest ground and cover your head. This is a last resort.

## Evacuation considerations
Evacuating far from a tornado is rarely possible. Instead, move to a sturdy shelter. Residents of mobile homes should identify a nearby sturdy building ahead of time.

## Shelter considerations
Lowest floor, interior, no windows, with as many walls as possible between you and the outside. Avoid large-span rooms like gyms and auditoriums.

## Afterward
1. Stay put until the warning ends, then check for injuries and give first aid.
2. Watch for downed power lines, gas leaks, broken glass and unstable structures.
3. Use text messages rather than calls. Do not enter damaged buildings.
4. Wear sturdy shoes and gloves when cleaning up.

## Common mistakes
- Opening windows to "equalize pressure".
- Sheltering under an overpass.
- Waiting until you see the tornado.
- Staying in a mobile home.
- Ignoring alerts at night.

## Special considerations for families
Keep children close and calm with a game. Assign each person a job. Keep shoes by the bed during storm season. Make sure schools, daycare and workplaces have shelter plans.

# flooding | Flooding
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: flood, floodwater, flood zone, cleanup, mold, evacuation, turn around don't drown
sources: nws, fema, cdc, epa, ready-gov
related: checklist:flood, guide:flash-floods, guide:unsafe-water-sources, guide:emergency-documents
summary: Floods are the most common natural disaster. Avoid floodwater, leave early when told and clean up safely.

## Understanding the threat
Floods can come from rivers, heavy rain, storm surge, dam or levee failure and snowmelt. Even a few inches of water can cause major damage. Moving water is powerful: **6 inches** can knock you down, and **a foot or two** can carry away vehicles. Floodwater is often contaminated with sewage, chemicals and debris.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Check flood risk for your address and your evacuation routes.
2. Buy flood insurance if you are at risk. Policies often have a 30-day waiting period.
3. Elevate or protect utilities, appliances and valuables. Know how to turn off electricity and gas safely.
4. Keep sandbags, plastic sheeting and a sump pump with battery backup if appropriate.
5. Store documents above ground level in waterproof containers.
6. Keep your car fueled and parked on high ground.

## Supplies required
Go-bags, water and food, boots, waterproof gloves, flashlights, radio, power bank, first-aid kit, medications, waterproof bags, life jackets if at risk, and cleaning supplies for afterward.

## Warning signs
A **flood watch** means flooding is possible. A **flood warning** means flooding is occurring or imminent. Rising water, heavy rain upstream, and local alerts are signs. Move quickly when a warning is issued.

## During the event
1. Move to higher ground immediately. Do not wait for instructions if water is rising.
2. Do not walk, swim or drive through floodwaters. **Turn around, don't drown.**
3. Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.
4. If trapped in a building, go to the highest level, not into a closed attic. Call 911 if you are in danger and signal for help.
5. If your vehicle is surrounded by rising water, abandon it and move to higher ground if you can do so safely.
6. Turn off electricity at the main breaker only if it is safe and the panel is dry. Never touch electrical equipment if you are wet or standing in water.

## Evacuation considerations
Leave early. Follow official routes. Bring pets and medications. Do not drive into water of unknown depth. Roads may be washed out beneath the surface.

## Shelter considerations
Use sturdy shelters or homes with friends outside the flood zone. Avoid basements and low areas.

## Afterward
1. Wait for authorities to say it is safe to return.
2. Avoid floodwater. It may be electrically charged or contaminated.
3. Wear boots, gloves and a respirator rated N95 or better when cleaning.
4. Throw out food and water that touched floodwater, including canned goods you cannot sanitize.
5. Dry the house quickly. Remove soaked carpet and drywall. Mold can grow within 24–48 hours.
6. Have electrical, gas and septic systems inspected before use. Treat private wells before drinking.
7. Document damage and contact your insurer.

## Common mistakes
- Driving through water.
- Returning home too early.
- Using electrical appliances that were wet.
- Mixing bleach with other cleaners.
- Ignoring mold, which can cause health problems.

## Special considerations for families
Keep children out of floodwater and away from cleanup. Floodwater can carry germs and sharp debris. Pregnant people and people with lung problems should avoid mold cleanup.

# flash-floods | Flash Floods
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: flash flood, canyon, low-water crossing, burn scar, rapid rise, arroyo
sources: nws, fema, ready-gov
related: guide:flooding, guide:wildfires, guide:roadside-emergencies
summary: Flash floods can arrive within minutes. Move to higher ground immediately and never enter flooded roads.

## Understanding the threat
Flash floods rise rapidly, often within minutes to hours of heavy rain, a dam failure or ice jam. They are especially dangerous in canyons, desert washes, low-water crossings, urban streets and burn-scar areas. Most flash-flood deaths involve vehicles.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Know if your home, route or campsite lies in a low area, canyon or dry wash.
2. Sign up for alerts and have a weather radio.
3. Identify the nearest high ground and walk-out route.
4. Avoid camping or parking in dry stream beds.
5. If a wildfire has burned upslope, learn about debris flows and flooding risk after rain.

## Supplies required
A go-bag, shoes, flashlights, radio, whistle, car emergency kit, waterproof bag for phone and documents.

## Warning signs
A **flash flood watch** means be prepared. A **flash flood warning** means flooding is occurring or imminent: move now. Other signs include heavy rain upstream, a sudden roar, muddy water rising, debris in streams, and a sudden drop in water followed by a surge.

## During the event
1. **Go to higher ground at once.** Do not wait for official orders.
2. Never walk or drive into moving water. Six inches can knock you down, and a foot of water can float many cars.
3. If your vehicle stalls in rising water, leave it and climb to higher ground.
4. Stay away from streams, culverts and storm drains.
5. Do not cross a flooded roadway to "save time."

## Evacuation considerations
There may be no time to pack. Leave with people, pets and documents. Move perpendicular to the flood path to high ground.

## Shelter considerations
Upper floors of sturdy buildings. Avoid being trapped in basements or garages. Do not shelter in vehicles in floodwaters.

## Afterward
Treat like a flood (see Flooding): avoid water, beware of contamination, and have utilities checked. Expect additional surges.

## Common mistakes
- Driving through a "shallow" flooded road.
- Camping in a wash during thunderstorm season.
- Assuming it is safe because it is not raining at your location.
- Going back to retrieve belongings.

## Special considerations for families
Teach children never to play in drainage ditches or storm channels. Plan routes for walking to higher ground with small children. Do not leave children alone near creeks after rain.

# severe-thunderstorms | Severe Thunderstorms
category: disasters
priority: high
tags: thunderstorm, hail, straight-line wind, derecho, damaging wind, severe weather
sources: nws, noaa, ready-gov
related: guide:lightning, guide:tornadoes, guide:flash-floods
summary: Severe storms bring damaging wind, large hail, lightning and flooding. Take them seriously even without a tornado.

## Understanding the threat
A thunderstorm is severe when it produces hail of 1 inch (quarter size) or larger, wind of 58 mph or more, or a tornado. Straight-line winds and derechos can cause widespread damage and power outages. Lightning and flash flooding are also major killers.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Receive alerts on a weather radio and phone.
2. Secure or bring in outdoor furniture, trash cans and trampolines.
3. Trim dead branches and remove hazardous trees near the house.
4. Know where you will shelter and keep shoes and a flashlight nearby.
5. Charge phones and power banks when storms are forecast.
6. Have a plan for outdoor activities: how to get indoors quickly.

## Supplies required
Flashlights, radio, power bank, water and food for outages, first-aid kit, tarp, and a safe indoor spot.

## Warning signs
A **severe thunderstorm watch** means conditions are favorable. A **severe thunderstorm warning** means a severe storm is occurring or imminent. Dark skies, rising wind, large hail, rumbling thunder and sudden temperature drops are clues.

## During the event
1. Go indoors. Stay away from windows, skylights and glass doors.
2. Avoid plumbing, corded phones and electrical equipment during lightning.
3. Do not go outside until 30 minutes after the last thunder.
4. If driving, slow down, turn on lights, and pull over in a safe place away from trees and power lines if visibility is poor. Do not stop under overpasses.
5. If hail is falling, stay in the car and cover your eyes if the windshield breaks.

## Evacuation considerations
Mobile homes are not safe in severe wind. Move to a sturdy building before storms arrive. Move people from tents, boats and open areas early.

## Shelter considerations
A sturdy building with an interior room on the lowest floor is best. Avoid large open-span rooms.

## Afterward
Check for injuries, downed power lines, damaged gas lines and tree damage. Do not touch fallen wires. Beware of flooding and watch for additional storms.

## Common mistakes
- Staying on the water, golf course or ballfield "just a little longer."
- Parking under trees.
- Assuming wind damage is not dangerous without a tornado.
- Using a generator improperly during outages.

## Special considerations for families
Know where children are, including at events and camps. Have stuffed animals or books for calming. Pets frightened by thunder may bolt, so keep them indoors with ID.

# lightning | Lightning Safety
category: disasters
priority: high
tags: lightning, thunder, strike, indoors, 30 minutes, when thunder roars
sources: nws, noaa, cdc
related: guide:severe-thunderstorms, guide:electrical-safety
summary: When thunder roars, go indoors. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back out.

## Understanding the threat
Lightning kills dozens of people a year in the U.S. and injures many more. It can strike up to 10 miles away from the storm. If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Check the forecast before outdoor activities and cancel or move them if thunderstorms are expected.
2. Know the nearest safe building or hard-topped vehicle.
3. Plan how long it takes to get everyone to shelter.
4. Appoint someone to watch the weather at group events.

## Supplies required
No special supplies, but a battery radio, weather app or alerts, and car shelter are helpful.

## Warning signs
Thunder, darkening skies, rising winds, static in the air, hair standing on end.

## During the event
1. **When thunder roars, go indoors.** A fully enclosed building or a hard-topped metal vehicle with windows up is safe. Sheds, tents, pavilions, dugouts and convertibles are not.
2. Stay inside and wait 30 minutes after the last thunder.
3. Stay away from windows, doors, plumbing, corded phones, and electronics plugged into walls.
4. Do not shower, bathe or wash dishes during a storm.
5. If caught outdoors with no shelter, avoid tall isolated trees, hilltops, open fields, water and metal objects. Crouching does not make you safe, and no outdoor place is safe in a thunderstorm.

## Evacuation considerations
Cancel outdoor activities early. Move boaters and swimmers off the water immediately.

## Shelter considerations
Large, enclosed buildings with wiring and plumbing offer protection. Cars protect because of their metal shell, not the tires.

## Afterward
- Lightning victims do not carry an electric charge. Call 911 and begin CPR if needed. Many survivors need care.
- Check for fires and damage.
- Unplug and inspect appliances that may have been affected by power surges.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting for rain before going in.
- Resuming activities as soon as the rain stops.
- Taking shelter under a tree.
- Using plugged-in devices or taking a bath during a storm.

## Special considerations for families
Teach children the rule. Coaches and event organizers should have a written lightning plan. Keep pets inside.

# winter-storms | Winter Storms and Blizzards
category: disasters
priority: critical
tags: winter storm, blizzard, snow, cold, power outage, travel, heating
sources: nws, ready-gov, cdc, red-cross
related: checklist:winter-storm, guide:extreme-cold, guide:hypothermia, guide:staying-warm, guide:ice-storms
summary: Heavy snow and cold cause power outages, road closures, hypothermia and carbon monoxide poisoning.

## Understanding the threat
Winter storms bring heavy snow, ice, high winds and dangerously low temperatures. A blizzard has sustained winds of 35 mph or more with low visibility. The biggest dangers include hypothermia, frostbite, car crashes, heart strain from shoveling, house fires and carbon monoxide.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Weatherproof your home: insulate, caulk windows and doors, and learn how to keep pipes from freezing.
2. Service heating systems and chimneys. Install smoke and carbon monoxide alarms with fresh batteries.
3. Stock food and water, extra blankets, warm clothes and a way to heat safely.
4. Keep your car full of fuel and equipped with a winter kit.
5. Have snow shovels, ice melt and sand.
6. Plan for pets and livestock.

## Supplies required
Water, food, flashlight, radio, extra batteries, blankets, sleeping bags, warm layers, first-aid kit, medications, backup heat source that is safe indoors, and a car winter kit with blankets, shovel, sand, jumper cables and a phone charger.

## Warning signs
A **winter storm watch** means severe conditions are possible in the next 12 to 48 hours. A **winter storm warning** means they are expected. A blizzard warning, wind chill warnings and travel advisories add risk.

## During the event
1. Stay indoors. Avoid unnecessary travel.
2. Dress in layers and keep dry. Close off unused rooms and gather in one room.
3. Check on neighbors. Eat and drink regularly.
4. Never use a generator, stove, grill or camp heater indoors. Use only heaters approved for indoor use, with safe clearances.
5. Shovel carefully, taking breaks. Overexertion can cause heart attacks.
6. If stranded in a vehicle, stay with it, run the engine for short periods with the tailpipe cleared of snow, crack a window, and tie bright cloth to the antenna.

## Evacuation considerations
If you must leave, wait until the storm passes and roads are cleared. Tell others your route and expected arrival time.

## Shelter considerations
Stay at home if it is safe, warm and has water. If not, go to a warming shelter or a friend's home. Keep one warm room.

## Afterward
Check for frozen pipes and roof damage. Clear snow from vents and exhaust pipes. Continue to avoid carbon monoxide hazards. Watch for ice and falling icicles.

## Common mistakes
- Traveling during the storm.
- Using generators or grills indoors.
- Overexerting while shoveling.
- Running out of fuel while heating a vehicle.
- Letting fires burn unattended.

## Special considerations for families
Dress children warmly and watch for frostbite. Keep infants warm without overheating or loose bedding. Check older relatives. Plan alternative care if schools close.

# ice-storms | Ice Storms
category: disasters
priority: high
tags: ice storm, freezing rain, power lines, tree damage, black ice, outage
sources: nws, ready-gov, fema
related: guide:winter-storms, guide:extended-power-outages, guide:generator-safety
summary: Freezing rain coats everything in ice, bringing down trees and power lines and making travel deadly.

## Understanding the threat
Ice storms occur when rain freezes on contact. Even a quarter inch of ice adds heavy weight to trees and lines, causing widespread, long-lasting power outages, impassable roads and falling branches.

## Preparing beforehand
1. Prepare for a multi-day power outage (see Extended Power Outages).
2. Remove dead or weak tree limbs near your house and power lines (have professionals do work near lines).
3. Have a safe backup heat source and carbon monoxide alarms.
4. Keep cell phones and batteries charged.
5. Stock rock salt or sand, and a way to clear ice safely.

## Supplies required
Same as winter storm supplies plus extra batteries, flashlights, blankets, fuel for safe heaters, and a battery or crank radio.

## Warning signs
A **freezing rain advisory** or **ice storm warning**. Temperatures near freezing with rain, ice accumulating on trees and lines, creaking branches.

## During the event
1. Stay home. Do not travel on ice.
2. Stay away from windows and trees that could fall.
3. Treat all downed lines as live. Stay at least 35 feet (10 meters) away and call the utility and 911.
4. Use safe heating methods only. Never operate generators indoors.
5. Keep faucets dripping and open cabinet doors if pipes may freeze, as long as you still have water.

## Evacuation considerations
Do not evacuate during icy conditions unless in danger. If you have to travel, bring winter gear and go slowly.

## Shelter considerations
Gather in one room, use blankets, and close off unused spaces. Know where the nearest warming center is.

## Afterward
- Watch for falling ice and branches.
- Do not touch downed lines, and assume they are live.
- Use caution walking on ice and use sand or salt.
- Check pipes and roofs. Food in the fridge may spoil during long outages, see the food safety guide.

## Common mistakes
- Walking or driving on ice unnecessarily.
- Going near trees or lines after the storm.
- Using a charcoal grill or a gas oven for heat.
- Trying to remove heavy limbs on lines yourself.

## Special considerations for families
Falls are the main injury. Do not allow children to play near fallen branches. People who rely on powered medical equipment should plan to move early if the outage is likely to be long.
