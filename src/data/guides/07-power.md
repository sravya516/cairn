# power-outage-planning | Power Outage Planning
category: power
priority: critical
tags: power outage, planning, backup power, utility, preparedness
sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc
related: checklist:power-outage, checklist:power-backup, guide:extended-power-outages, calc:power
summary: Decide what you must power, how long, and how, before the lights go out.

## Overview
Plan around essentials: medical needs, communications, light, refrigeration, heating or cooling, water and cooking. List them, estimate how long you need them, and pick the simplest reliable backup.

## Steps
1. Make a list of devices that must keep running (medical equipment, phone, router, radio, fridge, sump pump).
2. For each, find the wattage on the label, then estimate hours per day. Use the Power Calculator to total the watt-hours.
3. Decide how you will supply them: power bank, portable power station, solar charging or a generator.
4. Reduce loads: use efficient lights, turn off nonessentials, and prefer low-power devices.
5. Stock batteries, headlamps, lanterns and a radio.
6. Know your utility's outage reporting number and sign up for text alerts.
7. Plan to preserve food (see Refrigeration During Outages).
8. Plan for people on medical equipment (see Medical Supplies and Equipment).

## Preparation checklist
- Charge devices and power banks when storms are forecast.
- Keep gas in the vehicle (a car can charge phones, outdoors with ventilation).
- Put frozen water bottles in the freezer to help keep it cold.
- Keep a corded phone if you have a landline, since cordless phones need power.
- Know how to release your garage door opener manually.

>> Most households need power for lights, phones, a radio and refrigeration. Start there.

!! Never connect a generator to your home wiring without a properly installed transfer switch. It can electrocute utility workers.

# batteries-maintenance | Batteries: Storage and Maintenance
category: power
priority: high
tags: batteries, lithium-ion, alkaline, rechargeable, storage, battery safety
sources: cpsc, nfpa, epa, ready-gov
related: guide:portable-power-stations, guide:lighting-flashlights, calc:battery
summary: Store batteries correctly, rotate them and handle lithium batteries safely.

## Overview
Batteries run lights, radios and phones. They lose capacity over time and can leak or catch fire if mishandled.

## Types
- **Alkaline (AA, AAA, C, D, 9V):** inexpensive, long shelf life (often 5–10 years). They can leak, so remove them from devices that will not be used.
- **Lithium primary (non-rechargeable):** long shelf life, good in extreme cold.
- **Rechargeable NiMH:** good value. They self-discharge, so recharge periodically. Low-self-discharge versions hold charge longer.
- **Lithium-ion and LiFePO4 (power banks and stations):** high capacity. Follow manufacturer instructions.

## Storage and care
1. Keep batteries in a cool, dry place. Heat shortens life.
2. For long-term storage of lithium-ion batteries, store partially charged (around 40–60%) and top up every few months.
3. Do not store loose batteries in a pocket or drawer with coins or keys, which can short-circuit.
4. Keep batteries of different types or ages separate. Do not mix old and new.
5. Rotate: mark purchase dates and use older ones first.
6. Test power banks and stations every few months.

## Safety
- Use the charger and cable that came with the device or approved equivalents.
- Do not charge damaged, swollen or hot batteries. Stop using them and place them in a nonflammable container away from combustibles.
- Do not charge devices unattended on soft surfaces such as beds.
- Do not charge or store lithium batteries in extreme heat or in direct sun or in a hot car.
- Recycle used batteries according to local rules. Do not throw lithium batteries in household trash.

!! A swollen, hot or smoking battery is a fire risk. Move it away from flammables if you can do so safely, and call 911 if it ignites.

# portable-power-stations | Portable Power Stations and Solar Generators
category: power
priority: high
tags: power station, solar generator, inverter, watt-hours, LiFePO4, capacity
sources: cpsc, nfpa, ready-gov
related: guide:solar-panels, guide:power-calculations, guide:batteries-maintenance, calc:power
summary: A power station is a big battery with outlets. Size it by watt-hours and the watts you need, and never expect it to run everything.

## Overview
Portable power stations (often called "solar generators" when paired with panels) store energy in a battery and provide AC, USB and DC outlets. They are quiet, safe indoors (they produce no fumes), and good for phones, lights, CPAP machines and small appliances.

## Key specifications
- **Capacity (Wh):** how much energy it stores. A 500 Wh station can deliver about 500 watts for one hour in theory, in practice less because of conversion losses.
- **Output (W):** the maximum continuous watts it can supply. Surge ratings cover the startup spike of motors.
- **Battery chemistry:** LiFePO4 batteries last more cycles and are tolerant of heat; standard lithium-ion packs are lighter.
- **Charging:** wall, car, and solar input. Check solar input limits (voltage and watts).
- **Pass-through and UPS function:** some can power devices while charging, useful for medical devices that need stable power. Confirm with the manufacturer.

## Choosing
1. Use the Power Calculator to total your watt-hours for the devices you need.
2. Add a safety margin (for example 20–30%) and consider inverter losses.
3. Make sure the output is higher than the peak load, including the startup surge.
4. Check weight and portability.
5. Compare warranty and expected life cycles.

## Realistic expectations
- Phone charges: a typical phone uses about 10–15 Wh for a full charge.
- LED lights: 5–10 W each.
- Refrigerator: often 100–200 W while running, with a larger surge, and it runs only part of the time (see Power Calculations).
- Space heaters, electric stoves, hair dryers and air conditioners draw 1,000+ W. They drain stations very quickly. Avoid them.

## Care
Keep it charged to about 50–80% for storage and recharge every 3–6 months. Store in a cool, dry place. Keep the vents clear when running.

!! Follow the manufacturer's instructions. Do not use damaged units, and do not use any power station in a way that blocks vents or exposes it to water.

# solar-panels | Solar Panels for Emergency Charging
category: power
priority: normal
tags: solar panel, solar charging, foldable panel, charge controller, sunlight
sources: ready-gov, cpsc
related: guide:portable-power-stations, guide:usb-phone-charging, calc:battery
summary: Portable solar panels can recharge phones, banks and stations if you match voltage, watts and sunlight.

## Overview
Solar panels convert sunlight into electricity. They are slow but renewable, useful in long outages if you can recharge batteries during the day.

## Types
- **Foldable panels (10–200 W):** portable, with USB or DC outputs.
- **Rigid panels:** more durable, need mounting.
- **Panel + power station kits:** simplest, as they come matched.

## Matching and sizing
1. Check the input voltage and watt limits of your power station or bank. Never exceed them.
2. Panel ratings (watts) are for ideal conditions. Expect 50–70% or less in real use because of angle, haze, shade and heat.
3. Rough daily energy: panel watts × about 4–5 peak sun hours × 0.6 efficiency. A 100 W panel might give around 250–300 Wh on a good sunny day.
4. Use a charge controller if charging batteries directly. Use proper cables and connectors.

## Use tips
- Face the panel toward the sun, angled to the sky. Re-aim every few hours.
- Keep it out of shade, even partial shading cuts output a lot.
- Keep panels clean and dry. Secure against wind.
- Charge during daylight hours. Do not leave devices in direct sun, since they overheat.
- Test the setup before an emergency.

!! Do not connect panels to household wiring unless installed by a qualified electrician with a proper transfer switch.

# usb-phone-charging | USB and Phone Charging in an Emergency
category: power
priority: high
tags: phone charging, USB, power bank, cables, car charger, hand crank
sources: ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:phone-battery-conservation, guide:portable-power-stations, guide:solar-panels
summary: Keep your phone alive with power banks, car chargers, solar and smart habits.

## Overview
A phone is a communication tool, flashlight, map, radio and camera. Protect its battery during an outage.

## Ways to charge
1. **Power banks:** keep several fully charged. A typical phone battery holds about 10–15 Wh, so a 10,000 mAh (about 37 Wh at 3.7 V) bank gives roughly 2 to 3 charges in practice.
2. **Car charger:** charge outdoors or with the garage door fully open and the car outside. Idle only briefly in a ventilated area, and never in a closed garage.
3. **Solar panels and hand-crank radios with USB:** slow but unlimited.
4. **Portable power stations:** many USB ports.
5. **Computers and laptops:** can charge a phone but drain the laptop.

## Tips
- Carry the right cables (USB-C, Lightning, micro-USB) and a wall adapter.
- Use short, high-quality cables for faster charging.
- Charge everything before a storm, and unplug fully charged devices.
- Label power banks with the date you last charged them and recharge every few months.
- Use low power mode, lower brightness and airplane mode to extend battery.

!! Do not use damaged cables or chargers. Do not charge a phone under a pillow or on a bed.

# lighting-flashlights | Flashlights, Lanterns and Backup Lighting
category: power
priority: high
tags: flashlight, lantern, headlamp, candles, lighting, LED, glow sticks
sources: nfpa, ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:batteries-maintenance, guide:fire-prevention-escape, checklist:power-outage
summary: Use LED flashlights, headlamps and lanterns instead of candles. Keep light in every room.

## Overview
Darkness raises the risk of falls, mistakes and panic. LED lights are efficient, bright and safe.

## Choices
- **Headlamps:** hands-free. Great for cooking, repairs and first aid.
- **Flashlights:** one per person near the bed. Keep spare batteries.
- **LED lanterns:** light a room. Battery or rechargeable models.
- **Solar and crank lights:** no batteries needed.
- **Glow sticks:** safe light for children and for marking exits. Single use.
- **Candles:** risky. If you must use them, use sturdy holders on non-flammable surfaces, away from curtains, never unattended, and never near oxygen.

## Planning
1. Put a flashlight in the bedroom, kitchen, bathroom and near the main exit.
2. Store batteries separately or in lights with plastic tabs inserted to prevent drain.
3. Check batteries twice a year.
4. Choose lights with long runtimes and low-power modes. A bright setting uses much more battery than a low setting.
5. Use red-light modes to preserve night vision.

## Safety
- Never use an open flame near gas or fuel. Do not light matches if you smell gas.
- Keep lights out of the reach of toddlers if batteries are small button cells, which are dangerous if swallowed.

!! House fires from candles spike during outages. Choose LED lights whenever possible.

# generator-safety | Generator Safety
category: power
priority: critical
tags: generator, carbon monoxide, backfeed, transfer switch, grounding, fuel, portable generator
sources: cpsc, cdc, osha, nfpa
related: guide:ventilation-co-safety, guide:fuel-storage-safety, guide:electrical-safety
summary: Generators save lives and cause deaths. Run them outdoors, away from the house, with CO alarms in the home and correct electrical connections.

## Overview
Portable generators produce carbon monoxide, electricity and heat. Misuse is a leading cause of death after storms.

## Setup and operation
1. Read the manual and learn it before an emergency.
2. Run it **outdoors only**, at least **20 feet (6 m)** from the home, with the exhaust directed away from windows, doors and vents. Never in a garage, even with the door open, or in a basement, crawl space or enclosed area.
3. Keep it on a flat, dry surface under a generator-rated canopy or cover that does not trap exhaust, to avoid rain and electrical shock.
4. Install **battery-powered CO alarms** in the house.
5. Plug appliances directly into the generator with heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cords, in good condition and sized for the load.
6. Do not overload the generator.
7. Let it cool before refueling. **Turn it off and let it cool** before adding fuel, and store fuel properly.

## Connecting to your home
- Never plug a generator into a wall outlet ("backfeeding"). It can electrocute utility workers and neighbors and damage the generator.
- Have a qualified electrician install a transfer switch or interlock device to connect a generator to the home's wiring.

## Maintenance
- Run it monthly for a short time and keep the fuel fresh. Use stabilizer if recommended.
- Change oil and filters per the manual. Store it dry.

## If you feel ill
If you feel dizzy, headache, nauseous or weak while the generator runs, go outside to fresh air immediately and call 911.

!! Never run a generator inside any building, including a garage, and do not rely on open doors or fans.

# fuel-storage-safety | Fuel Storage Safety
category: power
priority: high
tags: fuel, gasoline, propane, storage, containers, fire safety, stabilizer
sources: nfpa, cpsc, epa
related: guide:generator-safety, guide:cooking-no-power, guide:fuel-and-routes
summary: Store fuel in approved containers, outdoors, away from ignition sources and in amounts allowed by local code.

## Overview
Fuel is dangerous. Spills, vapors and static sparks can cause fires and explosions. Storage rules vary by locality.

## Rules of thumb
1. Use **approved fuel containers** labeled for the fuel type. Never use glass, milk jugs or unapproved containers.
2. Store outdoors in a detached shed or ventilated place, away from the living space, water heaters, furnaces, pilot lights and other ignition sources. Do not store in the house or attached garage.
3. Keep containers closed, upright and out of the sun and away from children.
4. Fill containers outdoors, on the ground, not in a vehicle bed, to reduce static sparks.
5. Check local fire codes for the allowed quantities.
6. Label with the date and use stabilizer in gasoline if you will store it for months. Gasoline can degrade in about 3–6 months without stabilizer.
7. Rotate fuel into your vehicle regularly.
8. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby, rated for fuel fires (ABC or B).

## Propane
- Store cylinders upright outdoors in a ventilated area, not indoors, in basements or in vehicles for long periods.
- Check hoses and connections for leaks using soapy water.
- Never use a damaged tank or regulator.

## Spills and fires
Wipe small spills immediately and ventilate. If fuel ignites, call 911. Do not fight large fuel fires.

!! Gasoline vapors can travel along the floor to ignition sources. Do not fuel a hot engine.

# electrical-safety | Electrical Safety in Disasters
category: power
priority: high
tags: electrical safety, downed power lines, wet electronics, GFCI, surge, flooded electrical
sources: osha, nfpa, cpsc, ready-gov
related: guide:flooding, guide:generator-safety, guide:lightning
summary: Treat every downed wire as live, avoid water and electricity together, and have flooded systems inspected before use.

## Overview
Electricity is a hidden hazard after storms and floods. Contact with power lines and wet electrical equipment can kill.

## Downed power lines
1. Stay at least **35 feet (10 m)** away, about the length of a school bus. Assume every wire is live.
2. Keep others away and call 911 and your utility.
3. Do not touch a person or vehicle in contact with a line. If a line falls on your car, stay inside and call for help. If you must leave because of fire, jump clear with feet together and shuffle or hop away, without touching the car and ground at the same time.
4. Do not drive over downed lines.

## Water and electricity
- Never touch electrical equipment while standing in water or if you are wet.
- If water has risen near outlets or the panel, do not enter to shut off power. Wait for the utility or an electrician.
- Have flooded wiring, outlets, circuit breakers and appliances inspected and approved before turning power back on.
- Do not use electronics that were submerged.
- Use GFCI outlets and keep cords out of standing water.

## Overloads and fire
- Do not overload outlets or power strips. Use extension cords rated for the load.
- Turn off and unplug appliances when power goes out, so surges do not damage them.
- Keep a fire extinguisher (ABC) and working smoke alarms.

## Using tools
Use battery-powered tools or properly grounded equipment. Chainsaws, ladders and wet conditions cause injuries.

!! If you smell burning or see sparks or smoke in electrical equipment, evacuate and call 911.

# refrigeration-outages | Refrigeration During Power Outages
category: power
priority: high
tags: refrigerator, freezer, cooler, ice, outage, food safety, thermometer
sources: usda, cdc, ready-gov
related: guide:food-safety-outage, guide:power-outage-planning, checklist:power-outage
summary: Keep the fridge closed, use ice and coolers wisely and use thermometers to decide what is safe.

## Overview
Foods that need refrigeration must be kept at or below 40°F (4°C). A closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds for about 48 hours, and a half-full freezer for about 24 hours.

## Before an outage
1. Set refrigerator to 40°F (4°C) or below and freezer to 0°F (-18°C) or below. Use appliance thermometers.
2. Freeze water bottles, gel packs and extra containers. Freezing foods you will not use soon, such as leftovers and meat.
3. Fill gaps in the freezer with ice or water containers.
4. Stock coolers. Identify where to buy ice.

## During an outage
1. Keep doors closed.
2. After 4 hours, move perishable food from the refrigerator to a cooler with ice or frozen gel packs, keeping it at 40°F or below.
3. Group frozen foods together in the freezer.
4. Use a thermometer to check. Do not rely on appearance or smell.
5. Use dry ice with care: wear gloves, ventilate, do not seal in an airtight container and do not store in a closed vehicle.

## Medications
Insulin and some other medications must be kept cold. Check the label and ask a pharmacist how long they last at room temperature. Keep them in a small cooler with ice packs, not touching ice directly.

## Using a generator or power station
A modern refrigerator may run on 100–200 watts, with a startup surge. Only run it for a few hours at a time if power is limited, keeping it cold. See the Power Calculations guide.

!! Dry ice releases carbon dioxide. Do not use in enclosed spaces.

# power-calculations | Calculating Your Household Power Requirements
category: power
priority: high
tags: watts, watt-hours, runtime, power calculation, amps, volts, battery capacity
sources: ready-gov, cpsc
related: calc:power, calc:battery, guide:portable-power-stations, guide:solar-panels
summary: Learn how watts, watt-hours and runtime work, with realistic examples, so you can size backup power.

## Overview
Power math is simple. Once you can do it, you can compare devices and choose batteries or generators without guessing.

## Key terms
- **Watt (W):** how fast a device uses energy at one moment.
- **Watt-hour (Wh):** energy used over time. Watts × hours = watt-hours.
- **Amp-hour (Ah) and volts (V):** Wh = Ah × V. A 12 V, 100 Ah battery stores about 1,200 Wh.
- **mAh to Wh:** Wh = mAh ÷ 1000 × V. A 10,000 mAh phone bank at 3.7 V holds about 37 Wh.

## Steps to size a backup system
1. List each device and its running watts (check the label or a plug-in meter).
2. Estimate hours per day of use.
3. Energy per device = watts × hours. Add them to get daily watt-hours.
4. Add a reserve (20–30%) for losses and surprises.
5. Divide by inverter efficiency, often about 85–90%, if using AC outlets.
6. For batteries, only some capacity is usable (for example 80–90% for lithium types), so divide by that too.

## Example daily loads (approximate)
- Phone charge: 10–15 Wh. LED bulb (8 W for 5 hours): 40 Wh.
- Wi-Fi router (10 W for 8 hours): 80 Wh. Laptop (50 W for 4 hours): 200 Wh.
- CPAP (30–60 W for 8 hours): about 240–480 Wh, less with humidifier off, so check your device.
- Refrigerator (150 W running about one-third of the time over 24 hours): around 1,200 Wh.
- Space heater (1,500 W): 1,500 Wh for each hour, far too much for most batteries.

## Surges
Motors and compressors draw several times their running watts for a moment. Make sure your power station's surge rating covers this.
