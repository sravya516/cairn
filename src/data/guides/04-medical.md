# first-aid-kit | Build a First-Aid Kit
category: medical
priority: high
tags: first aid kit, supplies, bandages, medical supplies, trauma kit
sources: red-cross, ready-gov, cdc
related: checklist:first-aid-kit, guide:bleeding-control, guide:medical-supplies-planning
summary: What to include in a household and travel first-aid kit, and how to keep it ready.

## Overview
A well-stocked kit and basic training let you treat minor injuries and manage serious ones until help arrives. Keep kits at home, in each vehicle and in go-bags.

## Core contents
- Adhesive bandages in several sizes, sterile gauze pads and rolls, adhesive tape, elastic wrap bandage.
- Triangular bandages and safety pins for slings.
- Nonlatex gloves (several pairs), face masks, and a CPR face shield or mask.
- Antiseptic wipes, saline for flushing wounds and eyes, antibiotic ointment, hydrocortisone cream.
- Tweezers, scissors, thermometer, instant cold packs, emergency blanket.
- Tourniquet and hemostatic gauze, only if you have been trained to use them, plus compression bandages.
- Splint materials (such as a moldable splint) and a small flashlight.
- A printed first-aid guide, a list of emergency numbers and medical information for each person.

## Medications and personal items
Include only items that are right for your household and age group. Examples: pain and fever relievers, antihistamine, antidiarrheal, oral rehydration salts, personal prescriptions and any epinephrine auto-injectors. Follow package labels and your healthcare provider's advice on use. Do not give aspirin to children.

## Maintain it
1. Check the kit every six months.
2. Replace expired items and anything used.
3. Make sure everyone knows where it is.
4. Take a first-aid and CPR course from a certified provider.

!! This app does not give medication doses. Follow the label, your clinician and the poison help or 911 dispatcher.

# bleeding-control | Controlling Severe Bleeding
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: bleeding, hemorrhage, pressure, tourniquet, wound packing, Stop the Bleed
sources: red-cross, aha, ready-gov
related: guide:wounds-dressing, guide:shock, checklist:first-aid-kit
summary: Call 911, apply firm direct pressure, and use a tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure does not control.

## Overview
Severe bleeding can cause death within minutes. Quick action by bystanders saves lives. The steps below follow widely taught "Stop the Bleed" principles. Training makes you much more effective.

## Steps
1. Call 911 (U.S.) or ask someone to. Put on gloves if you have them.
2. Expose the wound and find the source of the bleeding.
3. Apply firm, direct pressure with a clean cloth, gauze or clothing. Push hard with both hands.
4. Do not remove the cloth if it soaks through. Add more on top and keep pressing.
5. If bleeding is from an arm or leg and is life-threatening, or pressure does not control it, apply a **tourniquet** about 2–3 inches above the wound (closer to the body), not on a joint. Tighten until the bleeding stops.
6. Note the time the tourniquet was applied. Do not loosen or remove it. Medical professionals will do that.
7. For deep wounds in areas where a tourniquet cannot go (groin, armpit, neck), trained responders may pack the wound with gauze or hemostatic gauze and hold pressure. Do this only if trained.
8. Keep the person lying down and warm. Watch for shock.

## Do not
- Do not remove embedded objects. Pad around them and apply pressure around, not on, the object.
- Do not use a tourniquet for minor bleeding.
- Do not wash a severe wound while the person is bleeding heavily.
- Do not apply a tourniquet over a joint or use thin cords.

!! If the person becomes pale, cold, sweaty, confused or faint, treat for shock and keep calling for help. Tourniquets are painful. That is expected.

# wounds-dressing | Wound Cleaning and Dressing
category: medical
priority: high
tags: wound, cuts, abrasions, infection, antiseptic, stitches, dressing
sources: red-cross, cdc
related: guide:bleeding-control, guide:infection-prevention-hygiene, checklist:first-aid-kit
summary: Clean and cover minor wounds properly and recognize signs that need medical care.

## Overview
Minor cuts and scrapes heal well with proper care. In a disaster, infection risk increases with dirt, floodwater and limited hygiene.

## Steps for minor wounds
1. Wash your hands with soap and safe water and put on gloves if available.
2. Stop bleeding with gentle direct pressure for several minutes.
3. Rinse the wound with clean running water or sterile saline. Remove visible dirt with clean tweezers.
4. Clean around the wound with mild soap. Avoid putting harsh chemicals directly in deep wounds.
5. Apply a thin layer of antibiotic ointment if you have it.
6. Cover with a clean nonstick dressing or bandage. Change daily or when wet or dirty.
7. Check for signs of infection every day.

## Get medical care for
- Wounds that are deep, gaping, longer than about half an inch, on the face or over a joint, or that will not stop bleeding.
- Animal or human bites, puncture wounds, or wounds with embedded dirt or debris.
- Any wound exposed to floodwater or sewage.
- Signs of infection: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaks, fever.
- You have not had a tetanus shot recently (check with a clinician about your schedule).

## Do not
- Do not use hydrogen peroxide or alcohol repeatedly in a healing wound.
- Do not close deep wounds with tape or glue if you suspect dirt inside.
- Do not apply butter, powders or home remedies.

!! Wounds from floodwater, rusty metal or animals carry higher infection risk. Seek care as soon as possible.

# burns | Treating Burns
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: burns, scald, blisters, chemical burn, electrical burn, cooling
sources: red-cross, cdc, nfpa
related: guide:shock, guide:fire-prevention-escape, checklist:first-aid-kit
summary: Cool the burn with cool running water, protect it, and know which burns need emergency care.

## Overview
Burns come from heat, chemicals, electricity and the sun. They are common after fires, cooking accidents, hot liquids and stoves in disasters.

## First aid for heat burns
1. Make sure the scene is safe and stop the burning. Remove the person from the source.
2. Remove rings, watches and clothing near the burn, unless stuck to the skin.
3. Cool the burn with **cool (not ice-cold) running water for at least 10 minutes**, up to about 20. Do not use ice, as it can cause more damage.
4. Cover loosely with a clean, nonstick dressing or clean plastic wrap.
5. Keep the person warm, especially children, and give pain relief only per the label and your clinician's guidance.
6. Watch for shock and breathing problems.

## Call 911 or get emergency care for
- Burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals, a joint, or a large area of skin.
- Burns that look white, leathery, charred or numb.
- Burns caused by chemicals, electricity or explosions.
- Inhalation injuries: soot around nose or mouth, hoarse voice, trouble breathing.
- Burns in infants, older adults or people with other health conditions.

## Special types
- **Chemical burns:** remove contaminated clothing and flush with large amounts of running water for at least 20 minutes while calling for help.
- **Electrical burns:** make sure the power is off before touching the person. Any electrical burn needs medical evaluation as internal damage can occur.

## Do not
- Do not put butter, oil, toothpaste or ice on burns.
- Do not break blisters.
- Do not remove clothing that is stuck to a burn.

!! Large or deep burns are life-threatening. Cooling a large burn area for long periods can chill the person, so keep them warm while you cool the burn.

# sprains-fractures | Sprains, Strains and Fractures
category: medical
priority: high
tags: sprain, fracture, broken bone, splint, immobilize, strain
sources: red-cross, cdc
related: guide:shock, checklist:first-aid-kit, guide:roadside-emergencies
summary: Immobilize suspected fractures, protect injured joints and know when to seek urgent help.

## Overview
Falls and debris cause many disaster injuries. It can be hard to tell a sprain from a fracture without an X-ray, so treat uncertain injuries as fractures.

## Signs of a fracture
Severe pain, swelling, deformity, bruising, inability to use the limb, a grinding sensation, or bone visible through skin.

## First aid
1. Call 911 for severe injuries, injuries to the head, neck or back, or if the person cannot be moved safely.
2. Control bleeding first with direct pressure, without pressing on exposed bone.
3. Keep the injured area still. Do not try to straighten it.
4. Splint in the position found, immobilizing the joints above and below the injury, using padded boards, rolled newspapers or a commercial splint.
5. Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth for about 20 minutes at a time to reduce swelling.
6. Elevate if it does not cause more pain.
7. Check circulation beyond the splint: warmth, color, feeling and movement.

## For sprains and strains
Rest the joint, apply cold wrapped in a cloth for about 20 minutes at a time, use a compression wrap that is snug but not tight, and elevate. Seek care if you cannot bear weight, the joint is unstable, or symptoms do not improve in a few days.

## Head, neck or back injuries
Do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger (fire, water, collapse). Hold the head still and wait for responders.

!! If fingers or toes become blue, cold, numb or tingling after a splint, loosen it and keep calling for help.

# shock | Recognizing and Treating Shock
category: medical
priority: high
tags: shock, circulation, faint, pale, clammy, injury
sources: red-cross, cdc
related: guide:bleeding-control, guide:burns, guide:anaphylaxis
summary: Shock is life-threatening. Recognize it early, call 911 and keep the person lying down and warm.

## Overview
Shock means the body is not getting enough blood flow to organs. It can follow severe bleeding, burns, serious injury, severe infection or allergic reactions.

## Signs
Pale, cool, clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; fast breathing; confusion, anxiety or drowsiness; nausea; weakness; blue lips; low urine output.

## What to do
1. Call 911 immediately.
2. Treat the cause: control bleeding, loosen tight clothing.
3. Have the person lie flat. If there is no suspected head, neck, back, pelvis or leg injury, raise the legs about 12 inches (30 cm).
4. Keep them warm with blankets or coats, but do not overheat.
5. Do not give anything to eat or drink.
6. Stay with them and keep talking. Monitor breathing and responsiveness.
7. If they stop breathing, begin CPR (see CPR and AED guide).

## Position adjustments
- If breathing is difficult, a sitting-up position may be easier.
- If vomiting or bleeding from the mouth, turn to the side if there is no suspected spinal injury.
- Pregnant people may lie on the left side.

!! Shock can develop after the injury looks under control. Do not wait for symptoms to worsen before calling for help.

# dehydration | Dehydration
category: medical
priority: high
tags: dehydration, fluids, oral rehydration, thirst, children, diarrhea
sources: cdc, red-cross
related: guide:heat-illness, guide:water-needs-calculation
summary: Replace fluids early, know the warning signs and seek care for severe cases or vulnerable people.

## Overview
Dehydration happens when the body loses more fluid than it takes in: from heat, sweating, vomiting, diarrhea, limited water supply or illness. It becomes dangerous quickly in children, older adults and people with chronic illness.

## Signs
- Mild to moderate: thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, less urination, headache, tiredness, dizziness, muscle cramps.
- Severe: confusion, very dark or no urine, rapid heartbeat, sunken eyes, fainting, no tears in babies, dry diapers for many hours.

## What to do
1. Move to a cool place and rest.
2. Sip safe water or oral rehydration solution in small amounts frequently.
3. For vomiting or diarrhea, continue small sips of oral rehydration solution. Follow the product label or your clinician's advice.
4. Avoid alcohol and very sugary drinks.
5. Continue breastfeeding or formula for infants and seek advice.

## Seek urgent care when
Severe symptoms, no urine for 8 hours or more, inability to keep fluids down, confusion, fainting, fever, blood in stool, or when infants, young children or older adults are affected.

## Prevention
- Drink before you feel thirsty, especially in hot weather.
- Do not ration water in ways that cause dehydration. Consume what you need and plan more supply.
- Check on those who may not ask for water.

!! If water is limited, do not use it for non-essential tasks. Protect drinking water for those who need it.

# heat-illness | Heat Exhaustion and Heat Stroke
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: heat exhaustion, heat stroke, heat cramps, hyperthermia, cooling
sources: cdc, nws, osha, red-cross
related: guide:extreme-heat, guide:dehydration, guide:staying-cool
summary: Recognize heat exhaustion early. Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency that needs 911 and rapid cooling.

## Overview
Heat illness ranges from cramps to life-threatening heat stroke. Older adults, young children, people who work or exercise outdoors, and people with chronic illness are most at risk.

## Heat exhaustion: signs
Heavy sweating, cool and clammy skin, pale skin, fast weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, muscle cramps, tiredness, headache, dizziness or fainting.

## Heat exhaustion: what to do
1. Move to a cool place, such as shade or an air-conditioned space.
2. Loosen or remove clothing and apply cool, wet cloths or take a cool bath.
3. Sip water if the person is alert and not vomiting.
4. Seek medical help if symptoms worsen, include vomiting, or last longer than about an hour.

## Heat stroke: signs
Body temperature of 103°F (39.4°C) or higher; hot, red, dry or damp skin; confusion, slurred speech, agitation or loss of consciousness; fast strong pulse; headache; seizures.

## Heat stroke: what to do
1. **Call 911 immediately.**
2. Move the person to a cooler place.
3. Cool them quickly with any means: cold water or ice baths if available, wet sheets, ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin, and fanning.
4. Do not give fluids if the person is confused or unconscious.
5. Stay with them until help arrives.

## Prevention
Stay in cool places, drink water, wear light clothing, avoid hard activity in the heat, never leave anyone or any pet in a parked vehicle, and check on neighbors.

!! Heat stroke can cause permanent harm or death within minutes to hours. It is always an emergency.

# hypothermia | Hypothermia and Frostbite
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: hypothermia, frostbite, cold exposure, shivering, warming
sources: cdc, red-cross, nws
related: guide:extreme-cold, guide:winter-storms, guide:staying-warm
summary: Get the person out of the cold, remove wet clothing, warm the center of the body and call for help.

## Overview
Hypothermia is a dangerously low body temperature, below about 95°F (35°C). It can happen indoors when the home is cold, and even in cool, wet weather above freezing.

## Signs
- Mild: shivering, cold skin, clumsy movement, fatigue, confusion, slurred speech.
- Severe: shivering may stop, drowsiness, very slow breathing and pulse, loss of consciousness.
- Infants: cold skin that looks bright red, very low energy.

## What to do
1. Call 911 for severe cases or if you are unsure.
2. Move to a warm, dry place. Handle the person gently.
3. Remove wet clothing and cover with dry blankets, including the head and neck.
4. Warm the center of the body (chest, neck, head, groin) first. Use warm (not hot) packs wrapped in cloth, or body heat.
5. If the person is alert and can swallow, give warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks.
6. Keep them lying flat. Do not rub arms and legs or use hot baths.
7. If they stop breathing, begin CPR and continue until help arrives.

## Frostbite
Signs: numb, cold, hard, pale or waxy skin. Move to a warm place, remove tight or wet clothing, soak in warm (not hot) water or use body heat. Do not rub or break blisters, and do not rewarm if it might freeze again. Seek medical care.

!! Do not give alcohol. Do not use direct heat such as heating pads or fires on frostbitten skin.

# anaphylaxis | Allergic Reactions and Anaphylaxis
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: anaphylaxis, allergic reaction, epinephrine, epipen, auto-injector, hives
sources: red-cross, cdc, aha
related: guide:shock, guide:special-diets, guide:medication-storage-continuity
summary: Anaphylaxis is life-threatening. Use epinephrine at once if prescribed and always call 911.

## Overview
Allergic reactions range from mild (hives, itching, runny nose) to severe and life-threatening (anaphylaxis). Common triggers are foods, insect stings, medications and latex.

## Signs of anaphylaxis
Trouble breathing, wheezing, swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, hoarse voice, dizziness or fainting, rapid weak pulse, widespread hives, vomiting or severe stomach pain, a sense of doom, combined with a likely trigger.

## What to do
1. **Use the person's prescribed epinephrine auto-injector right away** if they have one, following the device instructions. Do not wait to see if symptoms improve.
2. **Call 911**, even if symptoms improve. Reactions can return.
3. Have the person lie flat with legs raised. If they are struggling to breathe, let them sit up. If vomiting, turn them to the side.
4. If symptoms do not improve and another dose is available, follow the prescriber's and dispatcher's instructions on a second dose.
5. Keep the person calm and still. Do not let them stand or walk suddenly.
6. Monitor breathing. If they stop breathing, begin CPR.
7. Give the used auto-injector to the responders.

## Preparation
- Keep epinephrine where it can be reached quickly, and carry it with you. Check the expiry date and the liquid's clarity. Store at room temperature, away from extreme heat or cold.
- Wear medical ID if you have severe allergies.
- Teach family and caregivers how to use the device.
- Keep an allergy action plan from your clinician in your go-bag.

!! Antihistamines alone are not a treatment for anaphylaxis. Epinephrine is the first-line treatment.

# choking | Choking
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: choking, Heimlich, back blows, abdominal thrusts, infant choking, airway
sources: red-cross, aha
related: guide:cpr-aed, guide:children-preparedness
summary: Recognize severe choking and act at once: call 911 and use back blows and thrusts as trained.

## Overview
Choking happens when the airway is blocked. Severe choking is an emergency: the person cannot speak, cough forcefully or breathe.

## Signs of severe choking
Cannot speak or cry, cannot cough or has a weak, silent cough, high-pitched sounds or none at all, grasping the throat, blue lips or skin, panic.

## Adult or child (over 1 year)
If the person can cough forcefully, encourage coughing and stay with them.
If they cannot cough, speak or breathe:
1. Have someone call 911.
2. Stand behind the person. The **American Red Cross** teaches **5 back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, followed by **5 abdominal thrusts**, repeating. The **American Heart Association** teaches abdominal thrusts. Both are accepted; take a certified course to learn the method you will use.
3. For abdominal thrusts, make a fist just above the belly button, grasp it with your other hand and pull sharply inward and upward.
4. Continue until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive.
5. If the person becomes unresponsive, lower them to the ground, call 911 if not yet done, and start CPR. Look in the mouth before giving breaths and remove an object you can see. Do not do blind finger sweeps.

## Infant (under 1 year)
1. Have someone call 911.
2. Place the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting the head and jaw, with the head lower than the chest.
3. Give **5 back blows** with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades.
4. Turn the infant face-up and give **5 chest thrusts** with two fingers in the center of the chest.
5. Repeat until the object comes out or the infant becomes unresponsive. Then start infant CPR.

## Special cases
- Pregnant or large-bodied persons: do chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts.
- If you are alone: call 911, then give yourself abdominal thrusts using a firm surface such as a chair back.

!! Even if the object comes out, the person should be checked by a medical professional, especially after abdominal thrusts.

# cpr-aed | CPR and AED Reference
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: CPR, AED, cardiac arrest, defibrillator, chest compressions, hands-only
sources: aha, red-cross
related: guide:choking, guide:stroke-heart-attack, guide:shock
summary: A quick reference for hands-only CPR and AED use. This is not a substitute for a certified course.

## Overview
When someone's heart stops, immediate CPR and a shock from an automated external defibrillator (AED) can double or triple their chance of survival. This guide summarizes widely taught steps. A certified course is the best way to learn.

## Recognize cardiac arrest
The person is unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping).

## Adult hands-only CPR
1. Check that the scene is safe. Tap their shoulders and shout.
2. Call 911 and ask someone to bring an AED. Put the phone on speaker.
3. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone, with your other hand on top.
4. Push hard and fast: at least **2 inches (5 cm)** deep but not more than about 2.4 inches (6 cm), at **100 to 120 compressions per minute**. Let the chest rise fully between compressions.
5. Minimize interruptions. Keep going until help arrives, the person starts to move, or an AED is ready.
6. If you are trained and willing, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions.

## Children and infants
- Cardiac arrest in children is often caused by breathing problems, so rescue breaths matter. If you are trained, give breaths (30 compressions to 2 breaths, or 15:2 with two rescuers).
- Child: use one or two hands, about one-third the depth of the chest (about 2 inches).
- Infant: use two fingers or two thumbs in the center of the chest, about 1.5 inches (4 cm) deep.
- If you are alone with a phone, call 911 first and put it on speaker while you begin. If you have no phone, give about two minutes of CPR before leaving the child to get help.

## Using an AED
1. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts.
2. Expose the chest and dry it if wet. Place pads as shown on the pads. Use child pads or pediatric mode for children under 8 if available. If not, use adult pads.
3. Plug in the connector if needed. Let the AED analyze. Make sure nobody touches the person.
4. If it says to shock, make sure everyone is clear and press the button. If not, resume CPR immediately.
5. Resume compressions right after the shock. Continue following prompts.

## Drowning, overdose and other cases
Drowning, suffocation and overdose need breaths as well as compressions if you are trained. Tell the 911 dispatcher everything you know.

!! AEDs are safe for bystanders to use. They only deliver a shock when needed. Do not delay CPR to look for a pulse for more than a few seconds.

# stroke-heart-attack | Stroke and Heart Attack Warning Signs
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: stroke, heart attack, BE-FAST, chest pain, warning signs, 911
sources: aha, cdc, red-cross
related: guide:cpr-aed, guide:shock
summary: Know the signs, call 911 immediately, and do not drive yourself.

## Overview
Both are time-critical. Treatment works best when started quickly. Disasters raise the risk because of stress, exertion (like shoveling snow), heat, and interrupted care.

## Stroke: BE FAST
- **B**alance: sudden loss of balance.
- **E**yes: sudden vision loss or double vision.
- **F**ace: one side droops when smiling.
- **A**rm: one arm drifts down or is weak.
- **S**peech: slurred, confused, or hard to understand.
- **T**ime: call 911 at once and note the time symptoms started.

Other signs: sudden severe headache, confusion, numbness on one side.

## Heart attack
- Chest pain, pressure, tightness or squeezing, sometimes spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back.
- Shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea, lightheadedness.
- Women, older adults and people with diabetes may have less typical symptoms, such as fatigue, nausea or back or jaw pain.

## What to do
1. Call 911 right away. Do not drive yourself or have someone drive you unless there is no other option.
2. Stay calm and rest in a comfortable position, often sitting.
3. Unlock the door and gather medications and medical history.
4. Follow the dispatcher's instructions. They may suggest actions such as chewing aspirin for a suspected heart attack. Do not take medication unless told to by the dispatcher or your clinician, especially if you have allergies, bleeding problems or stroke symptoms.
5. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED.

!! If help is delayed during a disaster, keep trying 911 and ask neighbors to help drive you to a hospital. Do not wait.

# poisoning | Poisoning
category: medical
priority: high
tags: poison, ingestion, poison control, carbon monoxide, chemicals, overdose
sources: cdc, red-cross, cpsc
related: guide:ventilation-co-safety, guide:medication-storage-continuity
summary: Call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) or 911, and do not induce vomiting.

## Overview
Poisoning can come from swallowed substances, gases such as carbon monoxide, chemicals on skin or in eyes, medications and contaminated food or water. Disasters raise the risk because of storage changes, cleanup chemicals and fuel-burning devices.

## What to do
1. Get the person to fresh air if the poison is a gas. Do not enter an unsafe area yourself.
2. **Call 911** if the person is unconscious, having seizures, struggling to breathe, or very drowsy.
3. Otherwise, call **Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222** (United States, free, 24 hours). Have the product container, the person's age and weight, and the time of exposure ready. If the line is busy or the area has no service, call 911.
4. Follow instructions. Do not induce vomiting or give anything by mouth unless told to.
5. For skin exposure: remove contaminated clothing and rinse skin with running water for 15 to 20 minutes.
6. For eye exposure: flush gently with clean water for at least 15 minutes.
7. Bring the container to the hospital.

## Carbon monoxide
Symptoms include headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, confusion and chest pain. Move everyone outside into fresh air and call 911. See the Ventilation guide.

## Prevention
- Keep medications, cleaning products, fuels and pesticides locked away and in original containers.
- Never mix cleaning products, especially bleach and ammonia.
- Install battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms.
- Do not use outdoor equipment indoors.

!! Do not wait for symptoms. Call right away, and tell the operator you have limited power or connection.

# medication-storage-continuity | Medication Storage and Prescription Continuity
category: medical
priority: high
tags: medications, prescriptions, refills, storage, insulin, medical ID
sources: cdc, red-cross, ready-gov
related: guide:elderly-preparedness, guide:medical-records, checklist:gobag-individual
summary: Keep a supply, store it correctly and have a plan for refills and for medicines that need refrigeration.

## Overview
Missing essential medication for even a few days can be dangerous. Plan before an emergency.

## Prepare
1. Ask your pharmacist and insurer about emergency refills. Many plans allow an early refill or a vacation override ahead of a declared emergency or hurricane.
2. Keep at least a week of essential medications with you, and more if your clinician approves.
3. Keep a printed list: name, strength, form, schedule, reason, prescriber and pharmacy.
4. Take photos of labels and keep your pharmacy's number.
5. Pack medications in their original labeled containers in a waterproof bag in your go-bag.
6. Rotate supplies so you use older stock first and watch expiry dates.

## Storage
- Store at the temperature on the label, normally cool, dry and out of sunlight. Heat and humidity can degrade medications.
- **Insulin and other refrigerated medications:** check the label and ask your pharmacist how long they can remain at room temperature. Keep cold packs and a small insulated bag in your kit.
- Keep medications away from children and pets, and in a locked place if needed.
- Do not leave medication in a hot car.

## During an emergency
- If you cannot get a refill, ask a pharmacist, an urgent care, a hospital or the Red Cross. In a declared emergency, pharmacists may have special authority to dispense emergency supplies.
- Do not share prescription medications or skip doses without advice. Ask a clinician or the poison help line if you must change dosing.
- If a medication gets wet, overheated or frozen, ask a pharmacist before use.

!! This app does not provide medication dosages. Follow the label, your prescriber and your pharmacist.

# medical-records | Medical Records and Medical Information
category: medical
priority: normal
tags: medical records, medical ID, allergies, health information, advance directive
sources: red-cross, ready-gov, cdc
related: guide:emergency-documents, guide:privacy-documents
summary: Keep concise medical information for each person so responders and shelters can help you quickly.

## Overview
During an emergency, responders and shelters may need quick access to medical information. A concise, current summary can save time and prevent errors.

## What to include for each person
- Name, date of birth, address, emergency contacts.
- Allergies, especially to medications, latex, foods and insect stings.
- Diagnoses, past surgeries, implanted devices, pregnancy.
- Medications with strengths, how often and why.
- Vaccination history, including tetanus.
- Doctors and pharmacy contact details.
- Insurance details and policy numbers.
- Advance directives, power of attorney for health care, and do-not-resuscitate orders if they exist.
- Special needs, equipment and communication aids.

## How to keep it
1. Keep a one-page printed summary for each person in the go-bag and wallet.
2. Use the medical ID feature on your phone, so responders can see it without unlocking.
3. Store a protected digital copy and update at least every six months.
4. Share with a trusted contact.
5. Ask your clinician about patient portals that let you download your records.

## Privacy
Medical information is sensitive. Keep paper copies in a secure place. If your phone is lost, consider who could read it.

!! Keep the information accurate. An outdated medication list can be worse than none.

# medical-supplies-planning | Emergency Medical Supplies and Equipment
category: medical
priority: normal
tags: medical equipment, oxygen, CPAP, thermometer, pulse oximeter, supplies
sources: red-cross, ready-gov, cdc
related: guide:first-aid-kit, guide:elderly-preparedness, guide:power-outage-planning
summary: Plan for equipment that depends on power or supplies, and stock useful monitoring and care items.

## Overview
Beyond a first-aid kit, some households need ongoing medical supplies and equipment. Plan ahead with your healthcare team.

## Useful items to consider
- Digital thermometer and spare batteries, blood pressure cuff, pulse oximeter, glucose meter and strips, if relevant.
- Oral rehydration salts, saline, wound care supplies, gloves and masks.
- Incontinence, ostomy and catheter supplies, diapers and wipes.
- Spare glasses, contact lens supplies, hearing-aid batteries.
- Inhalers and spacers, nebulizer backup, spare oxygen tanks following safety rules.
- Menstrual and sanitary products.
- A battery or manual backup for powered equipment (see Power guides).

## Powered medical equipment
1. Ask your provider about a backup plan for oxygen concentrators, CPAP, ventilators, powered beds, feeding pumps and dialysis.
2. Register with your electric utility as a medical-need customer, if available.
3. Know your equipment's power consumption and your battery runtime.
4. Know where the nearest hospital, dialysis center or shelter with power is.
5. Plan transport and contact numbers in advance.

!! Do not use a generator or fuel-burning device in a closed space. Oxygen supports fire: no open flames or smoking near oxygen.

## Overview
Disease spreads quickly when water, power and sanitation are disrupted. Basic hygiene prevents most outbreaks of diarrheal and respiratory illnesses.

## Key habits
1. Wash hands with soap and safe water for at least 20 seconds, especially after using the toilet, before eating or preparing food, after cleaning up and after caring for sick people.
2. If soap and water are not available, use hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol, but it does not remove visible dirt.
3. Use safe water for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth.
4. Keep food covered and cooked thoroughly. Do not prepare food when you are sick.
5. Dispose of waste properly (see Emergency Toilets).
6. Clean and disinfect frequently touched surfaces.
7. Cover coughs and sneezes. Stay home when sick, if possible.
8. Keep wounds clean and covered.
9. Keep up with routine vaccines. Ask about tetanus before cleanup work.

## Caring for sick people
- Isolate sick people when practical, ideally with a separate room and bathroom.
- Provide fluids and rest, and watch for dehydration.
- Use masks if available, and ventilate when it is safe.

## Cleanup after floods or storms
Wear gloves, boots and eye protection. Do not mix chemicals. See the guides on flooding for mold and contamination safety.

>> Pack soap and hand sanitizer in every kit. They are small and extremely valuable.

!! Get medical care for high fever, bloody or persistent diarrhea, signs of dehydration, trouble breathing, or a wound with spreading redness, pus or red streaks. Infants, older adults and people with weak immune systems should be seen sooner.
