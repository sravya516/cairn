# understanding-risks | Understanding Your Emergency Risks
category: general
priority: high
tags: risk assessment, hazards, local threats, planning
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: guide:family-emergency-plan, guide:prepare-72-hours, checklist:kit-72h
summary: Learn which emergencies are most likely where you live so you prepare for real threats first.

## Overview
Preparedness works best when it is specific. A household in coastal Florida, one in Kansas and one in rural Vermont face very different threats. Start by listing the hazards most likely in your area, then prepare for those first.

## How to find your local risks
1. List hazards that have affected your area before: floods, tornadoes, hurricanes, wildfires, earthquakes, winter storms, heat waves, power outages.
2. Check your county or city emergency management website and sign up for local alerts while you have internet.
3. Look up your flood zone, wildfire risk, and hurricane evacuation zone using official local or federal maps.
4. Ask neighbors, your landlord, or the local fire department which events have caused problems before.
5. Add household-specific risks: medical equipment that needs power, mobility limits, pets, a long commute, a home far from help.

## Almost everyone should prepare for
- Power outages lasting a day or more.
- Being unable to leave home for several days (storm, snow, civil disruption).
- Needing to leave home with little notice (fire, flood, gas leak, hazardous spill).
- Disrupted phone, internet, and water service.
- House fires, the most common home emergency.

## Turn risks into actions
For each top hazard, write down: how you will get a warning, where you will go, what you will need, and what could go wrong with your household specifically. The disaster guides in this app follow that structure.

>> Start with a 72-hour kit and a family plan. They help in nearly every scenario.

!! Official warnings and evacuation orders override any plan. If officials say leave, leave.

# family-emergency-plan | Create a Family Emergency Plan
category: general
priority: critical
tags: family plan, communication plan, household, drills
sources: ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:contact-planning, guide:meeting-locations, guide:evacuation-planning, checklist:evacuation, checklist:shelter-in-place
summary: A written plan that every household member knows: who to call, where to meet, where to go, and what to bring.

## Overview
During an emergency you may be at work, children at school, and phones overloaded. A plan made in advance lets everyone act without guessing. Write it down, share it, and practice it.

## Build the plan
1. Hold a family meeting. Include children and anyone else who lives with you.
2. Identify the likely emergencies in your area (see Understanding Your Emergency Risks).
3. Choose two meeting places: one just outside your home, one outside your neighborhood.
4. Choose an out-of-area contact everyone will check in with.
5. Write down phone numbers and addresses: work, school, doctors, insurance, utilities.
6. Plan two exit routes from every room and two routes out of your neighborhood.
7. Decide who is responsible for children, older relatives, people with disabilities, and pets, including a backup person for each.
8. Learn how to shut off water, gas and electricity, and which emergencies call for each. Only shut off gas if you smell gas or are told to; only the utility can restore it.
9. Know your school, workplace and childcare emergency plans and how they will contact you.
10. Put copies of the plan in each go-bag and share it with your out-of-area contact.

## Practice
- Run a fire drill twice a year and time how long it takes to get out.
- Practice sheltering in place for severe weather in your designated safe spot.
- Review and update the plan every six months and after any move, birth, or change in health.

## Include special needs
Medications, medical equipment, mobility aids, communication needs, service animals and dietary needs all belong in the plan.

>> Use the My Plan tab in this app to record household details, contacts and meeting places on your phone. Print a paper copy as well.

!! A plan that only lives on a phone fails when the battery dies. Keep a paper copy.

# contact-planning | Emergency Contact Planning
category: general
priority: high
tags: contacts, out-of-area contact, phone numbers, contact card
sources: ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:family-comms-plan, guide:family-emergency-plan
summary: Choose contacts, memorize key numbers and carry a paper card so you can reach people when phones fail.

## Overview
Local phone lines often jam during a disaster while long-distance calls and text messages may still get through. An out-of-area contact gives everyone a single point to check in with.

## Steps
1. Pick one out-of-area contact (a relative or friend in another state or region). Ask permission first.
2. Give every family member that person's number, and ask them to relay messages between you.
3. Put the number on a card in every wallet, backpack, school bag and go-bag. Children should carry a card they can show to an adult.
4. Memorize at least two numbers. Phones die, are lost or locked.
5. Add local contacts: neighbors, nearest relatives, your physician, pharmacy, veterinarian, insurance agent, landlord or property manager, utilities and your employer.
6. Add medical contacts: specialists, home-health or medical-equipment providers, and the poison help line (1-800-222-1222 in the U.S.).
7. Save an "ICE" (in case of emergency) entry in each phone, and know how to reach medical information on a locked phone.

## During an emergency
- Text rather than call when networks are busy. Texts use little capacity and may go through when calls fail.
- Keep calls short. Free the lines for emergencies.
- Do not call 911 to ask about the disaster. Use it only for life-threatening needs.

## Wallet card should include
Name, address, phone numbers, out-of-area contact, meeting places, allergies, medications, medical conditions, blood type if known, and doctor.

>> Update the card whenever a number changes.

# meeting-locations | Choose Family Meeting Locations
category: general
priority: normal
tags: meeting place, rally point, reunification, separated
sources: ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:family-emergency-plan, guide:contact-planning
summary: Pick safe, known places to reunite if you cannot return home or cannot communicate.

## Overview
If you are separated and phones do not work, everyone needs to know exactly where to go. Choose places that are easy to find, safe, and reachable on foot.

## Choose three places
1. **Right outside your home** for sudden emergencies such as a fire. Pick a fixed point, like a specific tree or the mailbox across the street.
2. **Outside your neighborhood** for when you cannot return, such as a library, school or place of worship about a mile away.
3. **Out of town** for regional disasters, such as a relative's home. Decide when you would use it and how you would travel.

## Details to settle
- How to reach each place by foot, bike and car, and a backup route.
- Who picks up children from school and where the school would send them.
- How long to wait before moving on, and how to leave a note.
- Address and phone number of each place written on the contact card.

## Leave a note
If you must leave home, leave a dated note in a known spot (or taped inside the front door) saying who was here, where you went and when. Avoid posting this publicly online, which can reveal that your home is empty.

>> Walk or drive to each meeting place once so children know it.

# emergency-documents | Protect Your Important Documents
category: general
priority: high
tags: documents, insurance, identification, backups, records
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: guide:privacy-documents, checklist:gobag-individual, checklist:recovery
summary: Keep copies of identification, insurance, medical and financial records where you can grab or recover them.

## Overview
After a fire, flood or evacuation, replacing documents is hard and slow. Gather copies now and store them in more than one place.

## What to collect
- Government IDs, passports, birth certificates, Social Security cards, marriage and adoption papers.
- Insurance policies (home, renters, auto, health, life) with agent contact numbers.
- Property deeds, leases, vehicle titles and registrations.
- Bank and credit card account numbers (partially masked on paper copies) and the phone numbers to report loss.
- Medical records, immunization records, medication lists, prescriptions and healthcare directives.
- Photos or video of home contents and valuables for insurance claims.
- Recent tax returns, wills and key household contacts.

## How to store them
1. Make paper copies in a waterproof, fire-resistant container or zip bag in your go-bag.
2. Make encrypted digital copies on a password-protected USB drive or phone, stored separately from the originals.
3. Give a trusted person out of the area a copy, or store it in a safe-deposit box.
4. Keep originals in a fire-resistant safe or deposit box, not in the go-bag unless you must evacuate.
5. Keep some cash in small bills and a list of key account numbers.

## Maintain it
Review every six months. Replace expired IDs and update insurance after major purchases.

!! Identity theft risk: do not leave full copies of IDs in your vehicle or share them online. Use the Export feature in My Plan only to storage you control.

# children-preparedness | Emergency Preparedness for Children
category: general
priority: high
tags: children, kids, school, family, comfort
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, fema
related: guide:family-emergency-plan, guide:food-children, checklist:gobag-family
summary: Prepare children with age-appropriate information, practice, comfort items and clear school plans.

## Overview
Children cope better when they know what to expect. Calm, simple, honest explanations and practice reduce fear and help them follow instructions.

## Talk and practice
1. Explain emergencies in plain words without graphic detail. Focus on what they can do.
2. Teach them how to call 911 and when. Teach their full name, address and a parent's phone number.
3. Practice fire drills, "drop, cover, hold on", and where to go for tornadoes.
4. Practice staying with the group and following adult instructions.
5. Teach them never to hide in an emergency and to stay with a trusted adult or first responder.

## School and childcare
- Learn the school's emergency, evacuation and reunification plans.
- Make sure the school has up-to-date emergency contacts and who may pick up your child.
- Ask how the school communicates, and how it handles medications and children with special needs.

## Kit items for children
- Comfort items: a stuffed animal, book, cards or quiet game.
- Extra clothing, diapers, wipes, formula and bottles for infants, baby food as needed.
- Child-sized medications and information about allergies.
- A recent photo of each child and a contact card.

## After an event
Expect changes in sleep, appetite, clinginess or behavior. Keep routines where possible and seek professional help if distress continues.

>> Let children help pack their own go-bag item list. It builds confidence.

# elderly-preparedness | Emergency Preparedness for Older Adults
category: general
priority: high
tags: elderly, seniors, older adults, medications, mobility
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, cdc
related: guide:medication-storage-continuity, guide:extreme-heat, checklist:gobag-individual
summary: Plan for medications, mobility, medical equipment, caregivers and extreme temperatures.

## Overview
Older adults are more vulnerable to heat, cold, disrupted care and power failures. A plan built around health needs and a support network keeps independence and safety.

## Build a support network
1. Ask at least two or three people nearby to check on you and know where your supplies are.
2. Give them keys, your plan and a list of medications and conditions.
3. Arrange transportation for evacuation if you do not drive.
4. Register for local special-needs or vulnerable-population notification if your community has one.

## Medical planning
- Keep at least a week of medications if your prescriber and insurer allow it, plus a written medication list with doses and the reason for each.
- Ask your doctor about backup plans for equipment that needs electricity (oxygen concentrators, CPAP, powered beds, dialysis).
- Tell your electric utility if you rely on powered medical equipment and ask about priority restoration programs. These do not guarantee power.
- Keep copies of insurance cards, Medicare or other benefit information, and doctor contact details.
- Keep extra eyeglasses, hearing-aid batteries, dentures and mobility-aid parts.

## Mobility and home
- Keep paths clear, flashlights by the bed, and a whistle or phone within reach.
- Know how to open garage doors or lifts without power.
- Choose a ground-level safe spot for storms if stairs are a problem.

## Heat and cold
Older adults may not feel temperature changes as strongly. Check on them often during heat waves or cold snaps and have a plan to move them to a cooler or warmer place.

!! Never depend only on one caregiver. Have a backup person.

# disabilities-preparedness | Preparedness for People with Disabilities and Access Needs
category: general
priority: high
tags: disability, access needs, mobility, deaf, blind, service animal
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: guide:elderly-preparedness, guide:family-emergency-plan
summary: Tailor plans for mobility, vision, hearing, cognitive and medical needs, including equipment, communication and caregivers.

## Overview
Emergency plans must fit your abilities and equipment. Think through each daily need and how you would meet it without electricity, normal transportation or your usual helpers.

## Steps
1. List the help you need daily: dressing, moving, communicating, eating, medical care.
2. Build a personal support network of at least three people and share keys and instructions.
3. Write a short information card describing your needs, how to assist you, and how to communicate with you.
4. Plan accessible evacuation routes and accessible transportation. Ask about accessible shelters and your local emergency management office's registry.
5. Carry extras: batteries for wheelchairs and hearing aids, a manual wheelchair backup if possible, charging cables and a power bank.
6. Label equipment with your name and contact details and keep copies of equipment instructions and warranty information.

## By need
- **Mobility:** practice evacuating down stairs with and without help, learn how evacuation chairs work, keep a lightweight backup device.
- **Vision:** keep spare canes, braille or large-print copies of key information and a talking or battery-powered radio.
- **Hearing:** use text-based alerts and wireless emergency alerts, keep paper and pens, and a flashlight for lip-reading or signing at night.
- **Cognitive or developmental:** use simple picture-based plans, practice drills, and carry comfort items and a care information card.
- **Service animals:** pack food, water, bowls, medication, vaccination records and ID. Shelters allow service animals.
- **Medical equipment:** see Older Adults guidance on power backup and utilities.

>> Tell first responders about your needs as soon as you reach them.

# pet-preparedness | Emergency Preparedness for Pets
category: general
priority: high
tags: pets, animals, dogs, cats, pet kit, microchip
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, fema
related: checklist:pet-kit, guide:pet-evacuation, guide:food-pets
summary: Include pets in your plan with food, water, records, carriers, identification and pet-friendly places to stay.

## Overview
Many people refuse to evacuate because of pets, which puts everyone at risk. Prepare now so your animals can come with you.

## Prepare
1. Make sure pets wear collars with ID and are microchipped, with current contact information registered.
2. Keep current vaccination records and a recent photo of each pet with you.
3. Identify pet-friendly hotels, boarding facilities, veterinary clinics and friends or family who can host animals. Most public shelters other than service animals may not accept pets, so check local rules.
4. Prepare a pet go-kit: food, water, bowls, medication, leash, carrier, litter, bags, blankets and toys.
5. Arrange who will care for pets if you are not home when an evacuation is ordered.
6. Practice putting pets in carriers and loading them in the car.

## Supplies
- At least three days of food, ideally more, in airtight waterproof containers, rotated regularly.
- Fresh water for drinking (see the water calculator).
- Medications and medical records, with a first-aid kit for pets.
- Crate or carrier for each animal, large enough to stand, turn and lie down.
- Cat litter and pan, waste bags and cleaning supplies.

## During an emergency
- Bring pets inside early during storms. Never leave pets tied outside or in a vehicle.
- If you must leave and cannot take pets, do not leave them tied or caged. Leave plenty of food and water and a note.

!! Heat inside parked cars can kill pets within minutes.

# neighborhood-preparedness | Neighborhood Preparedness
category: general
priority: normal
tags: neighbors, community, CERT, mutual aid, neighborhood
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: guide:community-resilience, guide:neighborhood-coordination
summary: Neighbors are usually the first help available. Build relationships and shared plans before you need them.

## Overview
After a disaster, professional responders may be stretched thin for hours or days. Prepared neighbors can check on each other, share tools and skills, and reduce harm.

## Steps
1. Meet your neighbors. Exchange phone numbers and note who has children, older adults, medical needs or pets.
2. Identify skills and resources: nurses, electricians, generators, chainsaws, ham radios, large vehicles.
3. Organize a short meeting or message group to agree on how to check on one another after an event.
4. Learn utility shutoff locations together and share information about hazards such as gas lines, large trees and flood-prone spots.
5. Take a Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) or similar course if one exists locally.
6. Plan how to support residents who cannot leave home.
7. Agree on a visible signal (for example a card in the window) to show "we are OK" or "we need help".

## During an event
- Check on neighbors only if it is safe to do so.
- Do not enter damaged buildings, move seriously injured people unless they are in immediate danger, or touch downed power lines.
- Share information from official sources, not rumors.

>> Start small: a text group and a shared list of who needs a check-in is enough.

# extended-emergency-planning | Extended Emergency Planning
category: general
priority: normal
tags: long-term, extended outage, grid down, resilience
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: guide:prepare-72-hours, guide:prepare-two-weeks, guide:prepare-30-days, guide:extended-disruptions
summary: How to scale preparedness from three days to weeks and what changes at each level.

## Overview
Most disruptions last hours or days, but some, like major hurricanes, ice storms, wildfires and earthquakes, can last weeks. Prepare in layers so each step builds on the last.

## Three levels
1. **72 hours:** water, food, light, medications, communications, first aid and a go-bag. Covers most short events.
2. **Two weeks:** bulk water or treatment, larger food supplies, power backup, sanitation, and a plan to cook and stay warm or cool without utilities.
3. **30 days:** as above plus long-term food, water sources, resource rotation, community cooperation and financial planning.

## Questions to ask
- What breaks first in my home (power, water, heat, medical supplies)?
- Can I stay put safely, or will I need to relocate?
- How would I get news, money, fuel and medications?
- How long can I keep food safe, charge devices and keep someone warm or cool?
- Who can I rely on and who relies on me?

## Priorities
Safety, water, medications, warmth or cooling, food, sanitation, communication, then comfort. Build in that order.

>> Add a little each month. A steady, affordable approach is better than a big rush purchase.

# prepare-72-hours | Preparing for 72 Hours Without Assistance
category: general
priority: critical
tags: 72 hours, three days, basic kit, first steps
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: checklist:kit-72h, calc:water, calc:food, guide:water-storage, guide:go-bag
summary: The baseline every household should reach first: three days of water, food, light, communication, medicine and safety.

## Overview
Emergency officials commonly advise being ready to take care of yourself for at least 72 hours. This is the foundation for everything else.

## Core supplies (per person)
- Water: about 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene, so 3 gallons for three days. See the water calculator.
- Food: non-perishable, ready-to-eat items you actually like, with a manual can opener.
- Light: flashlight or headlamp per person and spare batteries.
- Information: battery or hand-crank radio, preferably NOAA Weather Radio.
- Power: phone charger and a power bank charged ahead of time.
- Medical: first-aid kit, a week of prescriptions if possible, glasses, hygiene items.
- Warmth and shelter: blankets or sleeping bags, extra clothing, rain gear.
- Tools: multi-tool, whistle, duct tape, plastic sheeting, work gloves.
- Sanitation: wipes, garbage bags, toilet paper, hand sanitizer, soap.
- Documents and cash in small bills.
- Special needs: baby supplies, pet food, mobility or medical items.

## Steps
1. Buy or gather one category per week until you finish.
2. Store the kit in a cool, dry place that is easy to reach. Keep a smaller version in your vehicle and at work.
3. Put a reminder on your calendar twice a year to rotate food, water, batteries and medications.
4. Practice using the radio, can opener and lights.

>> A bin or backpack with everything together beats supplies scattered around the house.

# prepare-two-weeks | Preparing for Two Weeks Without Assistance
category: general
priority: high
tags: two weeks, 14 days, extended supplies, stay at home
sources: ready-gov, fema, cdc
related: checklist:kit-2w, calc:water, calc:food, guide:cooking-no-power, guide:emergency-toilets-waste
summary: Extend the 72-hour kit to cover two weeks at home: water, food, cooking, sanitation, power and medicine.

## Overview
FEMA and Ready.gov encourage preparing for at least two weeks when you can, because some disasters leave stores, roads and utilities down for that long.

## What changes at two weeks
- **Water:** about 14 gallons per person is a lot of space. Combine stored water with ways to treat more (see Water Treatment Overview).
- **Food:** a rotating pantry of shelf-stable meals, with a way to cook without power.
- **Sanitation:** a bucket toilet system with liners and cleaning supplies, soap, bleach, hand sanitizer, trash bags.
- **Power:** a power bank or station, solar or hand-crank charging, extra batteries and lanterns.
- **Heat or cooling:** safe ways to stay warm or cool for many days (see Shelter guides).
- **Medicine:** coordinate prescription refills with your doctor and pharmacist and keep a first-aid reference.
- **Morale:** books, games, comfort food, a routine.

## Steps
1. Use the water and food calculators to size your supplies.
2. Store water in several containers so a single leak does not ruin everything.
3. Choose foods you eat daily so rotation is easy.
4. Keep a written inventory and update it each season.
5. Test your cooking and lighting methods at home, outdoors or with ventilation.

!! Never use grills, camp stoves, generators or heaters meant for outdoor use inside your home, garage or near windows. Carbon monoxide kills.

# prepare-30-days | Preparing for 30 Days of Disruption
category: general
priority: normal
tags: 30 days, month, long-term, grid-down, resilience
sources: ready-gov, fema, usda
related: checklist:kit-30d, calc:budget, calc:storage, guide:extended-disruptions, guide:food-rotation
summary: A realistic path to a month of self-reliance: sources of water, rotating food, sanitation, finances and community.

## Overview
A 30-day disruption is unlikely for most households but possible after major events. Preparing for it also strengthens you for shorter outages and for financial hard times.

## Core strategy
- **Water:** you cannot store a month of drinking water for everyone comfortably, so combine storage with a plan to find and treat water (rain, nearby sources, filters, purification). Test your methods.
- **Food:** build a pantry of foods you eat and rotate them (the "store what you eat, eat what you store" approach). Add long-term staples such as rice, beans, oats, canned meats and vegetables, and freeze-dried items.
- **Sanitation:** large supplies of liners, bleach (unscented), soap, wipes and toilet paper.
- **Heat and cooking:** safe fuel supplies and equipment, stored correctly.
- **Power:** conservative use, rotating charging, solar charging.
- **Health:** extended supplies of essential medications (talk to your prescriber), first aid and hygiene items.
- **Money:** cash, emergency fund and copies of financial records.
- **People:** neighbor cooperation, shared skills and security through community.

## Steps
1. Reach the 72-hour kit, then the two-week level.
2. Add a few extra items to each shopping trip and track them in an inventory.
3. Use the budget and storage calculators to plan cost and space.
4. Practice a "camp-at-home" weekend with water, power and stove off.

!! Do not hoard medication or hazardous materials beyond safe limits, and do not store fuel unsafely. Follow local codes.
