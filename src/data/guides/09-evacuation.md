# evacuation-planning | Evacuation Planning
category: evacuation
priority: critical
tags: evacuation, evacuate, evacuation route, zones, leaving home, when to leave
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: checklist:evacuation, checklist:gobag-family, guide:go-bag, guide:fuel-and-routes, guide:pet-evacuation
summary: Know when to leave, where to go, how to get there and what to bring, before an order is given.

## Overview
Evacuation orders may give little time. Planning in advance helps you leave quickly and safely. Always follow official orders, and leave early when you can.

## Know your risks and zones
Find out if you live in an evacuation zone for hurricanes, floods, wildfires or hazardous materials. Learn your community's alerts and routes.

## Make the plan
1. Choose at least two destinations in different directions: a friend or relative, a hotel, or a public shelter.
2. Plan at least two routes to each. Practice driving them.
3. Decide when you will leave. Have clear triggers (an official order, a forecast, a fire nearby).
4. Make a plan for each person: children, older adults, people with medical needs and pets.
5. Arrange transportation if you do not have a car. Contact local emergency management about assistance.
6. Keep your vehicle tank at least half full.
7. Keep a go-bag for each person packed and a family checklist.
8. Choose a person outside the area to know your plan and destination.

## When the order comes
1. Leave immediately when ordered. Do not wait.
2. Wear sturdy shoes and clothes appropriate for the weather.
3. Take your go-bags, medications, pets, documents and phone chargers.
4. Lock the house. Turn off utilities only if instructed, and unplug small appliances.
5. Leave a note saying where you are going.
6. Follow designated routes. Do not take shortcuts that could be blocked.
7. Tell your out-of-area contact where you are headed.

## On the road
Keep the radio on. Stay together if possible. Do not drive through flooded roads, downed wires or smoke you cannot see through.

!! Official orders override your plan. If officials say leave, leave. Delay costs lives.

# go-bag | Go-Bag Preparation
category: evacuation
priority: critical
tags: go-bag, bug-out bag, evacuation kit, backpack, 72-hour bag
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, fema
related: checklist:gobag-individual, checklist:gobag-family, guide:emergency-documents
summary: A packed bag for each person lets you leave in minutes with what matters.

## Overview
A go-bag (grab-and-go kit) contains the essentials you need for about 72 hours away from home. Pack it before you need it, keep it where you can reach it, and refresh it twice a year.

## What to pack (per person)
- **Water:** several bottles or a filter, plus purification tablets. Aim for what you can carry.
- **Food:** non-perishable, ready-to-eat, such as bars, nuts, dried fruit.
- **Clothing:** change of clothes, sturdy shoes, socks, rain gear, hat, gloves, warm layer.
- **Shelter and warmth:** emergency blanket or sleeping bag, poncho.
- **Light and communication:** flashlight or headlamp, spare batteries, small radio, whistle, phone charger and power bank.
- **First aid:** kit, medications for at least a week if possible, glasses.
- **Hygiene:** soap, sanitizer, wipes, toothbrush, toilet paper, feminine hygiene products.
- **Documents and cash:** copies of ID, insurance and medical info in waterproof bags, plus small bills.
- **Tools:** multi-tool, duct tape, whistle, dust mask, work gloves, maps.
- **Family items:** diapers, formula, comfort items, pet supplies.

## Tips
1. Keep each bag light enough for the person to carry (children carry smaller loads).
2. Use a sturdy, comfortable backpack with a waterproof liner.
3. Store near an exit or in a closet, and keep a small version in your car and at work.
4. Check it twice a year: replace food, water, batteries and medications and swap clothing for the season.
5. Add a card with your contacts and plan.

>> Consider a "grab list" for items you cannot pack in advance, such as medications, phone and laptop.

# vehicle-emergency-kit | Vehicle Emergency Kit
category: evacuation
priority: high
tags: car kit, vehicle kit, roadside, jumper cables, winter kit, trunk
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, nws
related: checklist:vehicle-kit, guide:roadside-emergencies, guide:winter-storms
summary: Keep supplies in your car for breakdowns, delays and storms, adjusting for season.

## Overview
You may spend hours stranded in a vehicle or have to leave suddenly. A kit in the trunk makes that safer.

## Core items
- Phone charger and a power bank, flashlight with extra batteries, and a whistle.
- Water and non-perishable food, plus a few essential medications.
- First-aid kit and a fire extinguisher (rated for vehicles, ABC).
- Jumper cables or a jump starter, a tire repair kit or sealant, an air pump and a spare tire with a jack and lug wrench.
- Reflective triangles, flares or LED flares and a reflective vest.
- Multi-tool, duct tape, gloves, tow strap and a basic tool kit.
- Paper maps and a card with emergency contacts.
- Blankets, warm clothing, rain poncho and a change of shoes.
- Paper towels, wipes and trash bags.

## Winter additions
Shovel, ice scraper, sand or cat litter for traction, extra warm layers, hand warmers, a candle-free heat source, and a bright cloth to signal.

## Hot weather additions
Extra water, sun protection, and a cooling towel.

## Maintenance
- Check tires, fluids, belts, lights and battery before seasons change.
- Keep the fuel tank at least half full.
- Replace water, food and batteries every six months.

!! Do not leave medication, lithium batteries, lighters or aerosols in a hot car for long periods. Do not leave children or pets in a vehicle.

# roadside-emergencies | Roadside Emergencies and Being Stranded
category: evacuation
priority: high
tags: breakdown, stranded, flat tire, snow, highway, vehicle emergency
sources: ready-gov, nws, red-cross
related: guide:vehicle-emergency-kit, guide:winter-storms, guide:flash-floods
summary: Stay visible, stay safe from traffic and do not leave your vehicle unless it is safer to do so.

## Overview
Breakdowns, accidents, severe weather and floods can leave you stuck. Staying with your vehicle is usually safest, unless it is in danger.

## If your vehicle breaks down
1. Signal and steer to a safe place away from traffic, ideally off the road.
2. Turn on hazard lights. Set the parking brake.
3. If it is safe, put out reflective triangles or flares.
4. Stay in the vehicle with your seat belt on if you are on a busy highway. Get out on the side away from traffic only if it is safe to do so, and stand far from the road behind a barrier.
5. Call for help: roadside assistance, 911 if there are injuries or danger.
6. Give your exact location. Use mile markers, exit numbers or GPS coordinates.

## Stranded in a winter storm
1. Stay in the vehicle. Tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Raise the hood if the snow stops.
2. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour for heat, with the tailpipe clear of snow. Crack a window for ventilation.
3. Make sure your tailpipe is not blocked, since carbon monoxide can enter the car.
4. Keep warm with blankets, clothing and movement. Eat and drink.
5. Do not set out on foot unless help is clearly visible nearby.

## Floods
Do not drive into flooded roads. If your car stalls in rising water, leave it and move to higher ground.

## Hot weather
Park in shade if possible, keep the windows slightly open, and drink water. Never leave children or pets in a closed car.

!! Standing near highway traffic is deadly. Stay inside the car on a busy road unless it is unsafe.

# fuel-and-routes | Fuel Planning and Alternative Routes
category: evacuation
priority: high
tags: fuel, gas, route planning, detour, traffic, range, half tank
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: calc:fuel, guide:evacuation-planning, guide:offline-maps-navigation, guide:fuel-storage-safety
summary: Keep your tank at least half full, plan range for slow traffic and know more than one route.

## Overview
Fuel can run out quickly during evacuations, with long lines and closed stations. Traffic uses more fuel per mile.

## Fuel plan
1. Keep your tank at least **half full** all the time. Fill up when a storm or event is forecast.
2. Estimate your range: tank size × miles per gallon (use the Fuel Planning Calculator). Subtract about 20–30% for stop-and-go traffic, heat or cold, and heavy loads.
3. Plan fuel stops along routes. Fuel stations need electricity to pump.
4. Carry cash in case card readers fail.
5. Consider a safe, approved fuel can if local rules allow. See the Fuel Storage guide.
6. Turn off the engine when stopped for long periods, if it is safe to do so.

## Route planning
1. Choose at least two routes in different directions. Mark them on paper maps.
2. Choose routes that avoid flood-prone areas, narrow bridges and chokepoints.
3. Follow official evacuation routes, which may use contraflow lanes.
4. Share your route and expected arrival time.
5. Download offline maps for the whole route.

## Electric vehicles
Plan charging stops in advance, since chargers may be crowded or lose power. Keep the battery well charged and know the effects of cold and speed on range.

!! Never use a fuel container in the passenger area of a vehicle. Transport it outside, secured, and keep it away from heat.

# pet-evacuation | Evacuating with Pets and Animals
category: evacuation
priority: high
tags: pet evacuation, carrier, crate, livestock, shelter, animals
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, fema
related: guide:pet-preparedness, guide:food-pets, checklist:pet-kit
summary: Take pets with you. Plan where they can stay and how to transport them.

## Overview
If it is not safe for you to stay, it is not safe for your animals. Plan ahead since many shelters do not accept pets (service animals are permitted).

## Plan
1. Identify pet-friendly hotels, boarding, veterinary facilities and friends or relatives along your route and at your destination.
2. Keep carriers or crates ready, labeled with your name and phone.
3. Keep current ID tags and microchip registration.
4. Pack a pet go-kit: food, water, bowls, medications, vaccination records, litter, leash, harness, toys, first-aid supplies and a recent photo.
5. Practice loading pets into carriers and the car.
6. Do not leave pets behind. If you cannot take them, arrange for someone outside the area to take them.

## During evacuation
- Keep dogs leashed and cats in carriers. Frightened pets may bolt.
- Keep pets cool, calm and hydrated. Never leave them in a parked car.
- Do not release animals to fend for themselves.

## Livestock and large animals
Plan transportation, destinations, feed and water, identification, and contact local agricultural or animal emergency agencies. Evacuate early.

## After
Keep pets on leash or inside, since familiar landmarks may be gone. Watch for hazards, and check them for injuries. Contact a veterinarian as needed.

!! If you have to leave pets behind in an emergency, do not tie or cage them. Leave plenty of water and food, and notify rescuers.

# emergency-transportation | Emergency Transportation and Family Evacuation Procedures
category: evacuation
priority: normal
tags: transportation, no car, public transit, carpool, family procedures, accessible transport
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: guide:evacuation-planning, guide:disabilities-preparedness, guide:elderly-preparedness
summary: Plan how everyone gets out, including people without cars or who need help.

## Overview
Not everyone has a vehicle or can drive. Community plans may include buses, paratransit and shelters. Plan alternatives.

## If you do not have a car
1. Contact your local emergency management office to learn about transportation assistance and registries.
2. Arrange a ride with friends, neighbors or relatives and agree on a time.
3. Learn about public transit evacuation plans.
4. Know where to meet to be picked up, and how to reach pickup points.
5. Pack lightly and be ready to leave quickly.

## Family evacuation procedures
1. Assign roles: someone for each child, pet, older adult, documents and supplies.
2. Decide who rides in which vehicle, and keep convoys short with radios or phones.
3. Count heads before leaving and at each stop.
4. Keep a card with your family's names and contacts in each vehicle.
5. Agree on a rendezvous point if vehicles are separated.
6. Practice a home exit drill with timing.

## Accessibility
If you or a family member use a wheelchair, oxygen or other equipment, ask about accessible vehicles, and practice transfers. Keep a power source for equipment on the road.

## Alternatives
Bicycles, walking and public transit may be the only options in a major traffic jam. Plan a route out on foot and an emergency bike kit.

>> Tell neighbors who need a ride that you will help if you can, or ask them to help you.

# returning-home | Returning Home Safely
category: evacuation
priority: high
tags: returning home, re-entry, damage assessment, gas leak, structural damage, utilities
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross, cdc
related: checklist:recovery, guide:flooding, guide:wildfires, guide:ventilation-co-safety
summary: Return only when officials say it is safe, and check for hazards before you stay.

## Overview
Returning too soon puts you in danger from structural damage, gas leaks, fires, contaminated water or ongoing hazards. Be patient and cautious.

## Before you return
1. Wait for an official all-clear from authorities for your area.
2. Check road and utility conditions and bring supplies, including water, food, flashlight, gloves, boots and a camera.
3. Bring protective gear: sturdy shoes, N95 respirator, eye protection and gloves.
4. Travel in daylight when possible.

## On arrival
1. Look for damage: sagging roof, cracked foundation, leaning walls, broken windows, hanging debris, downed lines and flooded areas.
2. Do not enter if the building appears unstable, if there is flood water inside, or if you smell gas.
3. Do not use matches, lighters or switches if you suspect a gas leak. Leave and call from outside.
4. Walk carefully and watch for snakes, wildlife, nails, glass and unstable debris.
5. Photograph damage before moving things for insurance claims.
6. Have utilities checked before turning on electricity or gas. Do not use wet electrical equipment.
7. Check food and water. Throw away unsafe food. Treat tap water as unsafe until told otherwise.

## Cleanup
- Wear protective gear, ventilate, and avoid mixing chemicals.
- Dry out and remove wet items within 24–48 hours to prevent mold.
- Take frequent breaks. Do not lift heavy items alone.
- Use generators and tools safely.

## Mental health
Disasters are stressful. Rest, eat, drink water and ask for help. Watch for stress and sleep problems in children.

!! Do not use gas appliances or turn on the electricity until a professional has confirmed it is safe after flooding or damage.
