# drowning-water-rescue | Drowning and Water Rescue
category: medical
priority: critical
tags: drowning, water rescue, rip current, ice, swimming, pool, lifejacket, CPR
sources: red-cross, aha, cdc
related: guide:cpr-aed, guide:flooding, guide:hypothermia, guide:shock
summary: Reach or throw, do not go. Call 911, then start CPR with rescue breaths if the person is not breathing.

## Overview
Drowning can happen in seconds and often silently. It is a leading cause of death in children. A drowning person rarely waves or shouts. Never enter the water as an untrained rescuer. A panicking person can pull you under.

## Rescue without getting in
1. **Shout for help and call 911** (or have someone call).
2. **Reach** with a pole, branch, towel or paddle while staying on solid ground, or **throw** a life ring, cooler, ball or anything that floats.
3. Keep your weight low and stay anchored.
4. In floodwater or fast current, do not wade in. Six inches of moving water can knock you down.

## When the person is out of the water
1. Check if they are responsive and breathing normally.
2. If not breathing, **begin CPR immediately**. Drowning is caused by lack of oxygen, so **rescue breaths matter** more than in most heart emergencies. If you are trained, follow your course: give rescue breaths first, then compressions and breaths. If you are not trained, do hands-only compressions and follow the 911 dispatcher's instructions rather than doing nothing.
3. Use an AED if available. Dry the chest first.
4. If vomiting occurs, turn them to the side, clear the mouth and continue.
5. Keep them warm and lying down if breathing returns. Do not stop CPR until help takes over.

## Do not
- Do not try to drain water from the lungs. Do not do abdominal thrusts for drowning.
- Do not leave them alone.

## After any near-drowning
Anyone who has needed rescue, coughed or struggled to breathe in the water must be evaluated by a medical professional, even if they seem fine. Breathing problems can develop hours later. Seek emergency care for coughing, trouble breathing, chest pain, vomiting, unusual sleepiness or behavior changes.

## Prevention
- Supervise children constantly near water. Stay within arm's reach of young children.
- Use life jackets. Install four-sided fences and self-latching gates around pools.
- Learn to swim and learn CPR.
- If caught in a rip current, do not fight it. Swim parallel to the shore, then back in.
- Stay off thin ice. If you fall through, kick and pull yourself onto the ice, then roll away to spread your weight.

!! Cold water can cause hypothermia and make swimming impossible within minutes. See the Hypothermia guide.

# asthma-attack | Asthma Attacks and Breathing Emergencies
category: medical
priority: high
tags: asthma, wheezing, inhaler, shortness of breath, breathing difficulty, COPD, action plan
sources: cdc, red-cross, aha
related: guide:anaphylaxis, guide:choking, guide:medication-storage-continuity, guide:shock
summary: Help the person use their prescribed quick-relief inhaler, keep them calm and upright and call 911 if it does not improve quickly.

## Overview
An asthma attack narrows the airways. Disasters raise the risk through smoke, dust, mold, cold air, stress and missed medication. Anyone with asthma should keep an **asthma action plan** from their clinician and their rescue inhaler close at hand.

## Signs
Wheezing, coughing that will not stop, tightness in the chest, fast or labored breathing, difficulty speaking in full sentences, pulling in at the ribs or neck, anxiety and, in severe attacks, blue or gray lips.

## What to do
1. Move the person away from the trigger (smoke, dust, cold air) if you can.
2. Have them sit upright and stay calm. Loosen tight clothing.
3. Help them use their **own prescribed quick-relief inhaler** exactly as their action plan says. A spacer helps. Do not guess at the amount: follow the label and plan.
4. Stay with them and watch their breathing.
5. **Call 911** if breathing does not improve quickly, if they cannot speak or walk, if lips or fingernails turn blue or gray, if they are very distressed, or if there is no inhaler.
6. If a person is struggling to breathe and the cause is unclear, treat as an emergency. Check for choking or an allergic reaction (see those guides).

## Prevention in an emergency
- Keep rescue and controller medications in the go-bag with a copy of the action plan. See Medication Storage and Continuity.
- During wildfire smoke or ash, stay indoors with clean air. Use an N95 outdoors if you must go out.
- Avoid known triggers and treat colds promptly.
- Do not run generators or fuel-burning equipment indoors.

## Related breathing emergencies
Chronic lung disease (COPD), heart failure, infections and carbon monoxide can all cause sudden breathlessness. When in doubt, call 911.

!! Do not give another person's prescription medication unless it is prescribed for them. If someone has no inhaler and cannot breathe, call 911.

# diabetic-emergency | Diabetic Emergencies
category: medical
priority: high
tags: diabetes, low blood sugar, hypoglycemia, high blood sugar, insulin, glucose, confusion, diabetic emergency
sources: ada, cdc, red-cross
related: guide:special-diets, guide:medication-storage-continuity, guide:shock, guide:seizures
summary: Treat low blood sugar quickly with fast-acting sugar if the person is awake and can swallow. Call 911 if they are unconscious, seizing or cannot swallow.

## Overview
People with diabetes can have blood sugar that is too low (**hypoglycemia**) or too high. Disasters disrupt meals, medications and refrigeration, which raises the risk. **Low blood sugar is the more urgent problem** and can come on fast.

## Low blood sugar: signs
Shakiness, sweating, hunger, pale skin, fast heartbeat, headache, irritability, confusion or odd behavior (it can look like intoxication), and in severe cases, seizure or loss of consciousness.

## Low blood sugar: what to do
1. If the person can check their blood sugar, do so. If you cannot check and the person has symptoms, treat anyway.
2. If they are **awake and able to swallow**, give a fast-acting sugar: about 15 grams of carbohydrate, such as glucose tablets per the label, a half cup of juice or regular (not diet) soda, or a tablespoon of sugar or honey.
3. Recheck or wait about 15 minutes. If still low or symptoms persist, repeat.
4. Once better, give a snack with protein and complex carbohydrate if the next meal is far away.
5. If they are **unconscious, seizing, or cannot swallow safely**, **call 911**. Do not put food or drink in the mouth. If a trained person has a prescribed glucagon product, use it exactly as labeled. Place the person on their side.

## High blood sugar emergency: signs
Great thirst, frequent urination, nausea or vomiting, stomach pain, fruity-smelling breath, fast breathing, drowsiness or confusion. This can become life-threatening. **Seek emergency care.**

## Preparing for disasters
- Keep at least a week of medications and supplies, test strips, a meter and spare batteries, plus fast-acting sugar, in your go-bag.
- Keep insulin cool. Ask your pharmacist how long it is stable at room temperature.
- Wear medical ID and keep a written plan.
- Never skip or change insulin or other medication without medical advice. If you cannot eat normally, call a clinician or nurse line for guidance.

!! A person who is confused and has diabetes should be treated as having low blood sugar until proven otherwise.

# seizures | Seizures
category: medical
priority: high
tags: seizure, convulsion, epilepsy, fit, shaking, unconscious, first aid
sources: epilepsy-foundation, cdc, red-cross
related: guide:head-injury-concussion, guide:diabetic-emergency, guide:cpr-aed, guide:shock
summary: Keep the person safe, time the seizure, do not restrain them or put anything in their mouth, and call 911 when needed.

## Overview
A seizure is a burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. Most last one to three minutes. Convulsive seizures cause shaking, stiffness and loss of awareness. Some seizures are subtle, with staring or confusion. Seizures can be caused by epilepsy, injury, low blood sugar, heat, infection and missed medication.

## What to do
1. Stay calm. **Note the time** the seizure started.
2. **Protect the person.** Ease them to the floor if standing. Move hard or sharp objects away. Cushion their head with something soft, like a folded jacket.
3. Loosen tight clothing around the neck and remove glasses.
4. **Do not restrain them.** Do not put anything in their mouth. They cannot swallow their tongue.
5. When the shaking stops, **turn them onto their side** (recovery position) to help them breathe and let fluid drain.
6. Stay with them until they are fully alert. Speak calmly and explain what happened. Be patient: confusion and tiredness are common.
7. Check for injuries and for medical ID.

## Call 911 if
- The seizure lasts **more than 5 minutes**, or seizures repeat without recovery in between.
- The person does not start breathing or waking up afterward.
- It is their first seizure, or they are injured, pregnant, in water, or have diabetes.
- They have trouble breathing or the seizure happened after a head injury or in extreme heat.

## If they stop breathing
Start CPR (see the CPR and AED guide).

## Preparing
People with epilepsy should keep a week or more of medications, a written seizure plan, medical ID and caregivers who know what to do. Missed doses, stress, lack of sleep and heat can trigger seizures in disasters.

!! Do not give food, drink or pills until the person is fully awake.

# head-injury-concussion | Head Injuries and Concussion
category: medical
priority: high
tags: head injury, concussion, brain injury, blow to the head, unconscious, vomiting, confusion, falls
sources: cdc, red-cross, aha
related: guide:sprains-fractures, guide:seizures, guide:bleeding-control, guide:shock, guide:children-preparedness
summary: Any significant blow to the head can cause a brain injury. Call 911 for warning signs, avoid moving the neck and watch closely over the next day.

## Overview
Falls, collisions and falling debris cause head injuries in disasters and cleanup. A **concussion** is a mild brain injury that does not always show on the outside. Bleeding from the scalp can look dramatic but is often less serious than it looks. The opposite is also true: a person can look fine and still have serious bleeding inside the skull.

## Call 911 right away if there is
- Loss of consciousness, even briefly.
- Repeated vomiting, a seizure, or a severe or worsening headache.
- Confusion, slurred speech, unusual drowsiness or trouble waking.
- Weakness, numbness, loss of balance, or vision changes, or pupils of different sizes.
- Clear fluid or blood from the nose or ears.
- A deep wound, a dented skull, or an injury from a high fall or crash.
- Any injury to an infant, or a baby who will not stop crying, will not feed or has a bulging soft spot.

## What to do
1. If a neck or spine injury is possible (fall, crash, heavy blow), **keep the head and neck still** and do not move the person unless they are in danger.
2. Control scalp bleeding with firm, gentle pressure using a clean cloth. Do not press hard on a dented area.
3. Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth to swelling for about 20 minutes at a time.
4. Keep them resting and awake enough to talk. Watch for changes. If they vomit and have no neck injury suspected, turn them to the side.
5. Do not give aspirin or alcohol. Ask a clinician about pain relief.

## Concussion signs
Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, memory problems, sensitivity to light and noise, trouble concentrating and sleep changes. Symptoms may appear hours later. Someone should stay with the person for the first 24 hours and seek medical care if symptoms worsen.

## Recovery
Rest, then return slowly to activity. Avoid another head injury until a clinician agrees, since a second blow can be very dangerous.

!! Wear helmets when cleaning up after storms and when cycling or riding. Falls from ladders and roofs are common in recovery.

# eye-injuries | Eye Injuries
category: medical
priority: normal
tags: eye injury, chemical in eye, foreign object, flush eyes, vision, debris, eyewash
sources: red-cross, cdc
related: guide:first-aid-kit, guide:poisoning, guide:hazardous-materials, guide:wildfires
summary: Flush chemicals immediately, do not rub or remove objects stuck in the eye, and get urgent care for any serious eye injury.

## Overview
Eyes are easily hurt by chemicals, debris, smoke, blows and sharp objects. Cleanup after storms and fires raises the risk. Eye protection is the best prevention.

## Chemical in the eye
1. **Flush right away** with clean running water, lukewarm if possible. Hold the eyelid open and let the water flow from the inner corner outward for **at least 15 minutes**.
2. Remove contact lenses if you can, but do not delay flushing.
3. Call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) or 911 and get medical care. Bring the product container.

## Small particle (dust, sand, eyelash)
- Do not rub. Blink, or pull the upper lid over the lower lid.
- Flush with clean water or saline.
- If the object is stuck or discomfort continues, seek care.

## Blow to the eye
- Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth, **without pressing** on the eye.
- Seek care for vision changes, severe pain, blood in the eye or double vision.

## Cut, or object stuck in the eye
1. **Do not remove** the object. Do not press on the eye.
2. Cover the eye with a rigid shield, such as a paper cup taped over it, without pressure on the eyeball. Cover the other eye too to reduce movement.
3. **Call 911 or go to an emergency room immediately.**

## Smoke, ash and burns to the eye
Flush with clean water. Seek care for persistent pain, redness or vision changes.

## Prevention
- Wear safety glasses or goggles when cutting, sanding, using chemicals or cleaning debris.
- Keep eyewash or sterile saline in your first-aid kit.

!! Do not use eye drops, ointments or cotton swabs on a serious injury. Do not try to remove an embedded object.

# bites-stings | Animal Bites, Snakebites, Insect Stings and Ticks
category: medical
priority: high
tags: animal bite, snakebite, dog bite, insect sting, bee sting, spider bite, tick, rabies, snake
sources: cdc, red-cross
related: guide:anaphylaxis, guide:wounds-dressing, guide:bleeding-control, guide:infection-prevention-hygiene
summary: Wash bites, control bleeding and get care for deep or risky bites. Keep a snakebite still, call 911 and do not cut, suck or tourniquet it.

## Animal bites
1. Move away from the animal. Do not try to catch it.
2. **Control bleeding** with direct pressure.
3. Wash the wound gently with soap and running water for several minutes.
4. Cover with a clean dressing.
5. **Seek medical care** for deep bites, bites to the face, hands or joints, bites that will not stop bleeding, or any bite from a wild animal or an unknown or unvaccinated pet. Rabies and tetanus are risks. Report the bite to animal control or local health officials.

## Snakebites (U.S.)
1. Move away from the snake. Do not try to kill or capture it.
2. **Call 911.** Keep the person calm and still, since movement spreads venom faster.
3. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the bite before swelling begins.
4. Keep the bitten limb at or slightly below the level of the heart, loosely immobilized.
5. Note the time and the snake's appearance if it is safe to do so.
- **Do not** cut the wound, suck out the venom, apply ice, use a tourniquet or give alcohol or caffeine.

## Insect stings
- Scrape out a bee stinger with a card edge. Wash the area and apply a cold pack.
- Watch for signs of a severe allergic reaction. If breathing trouble, swelling of the face or throat, widespread hives or dizziness occur, use epinephrine if prescribed and call 911 (see Anaphylaxis).

## Spiders and scorpions
Wash the area and apply a cold pack. Seek care for severe pain, spreading redness, muscle cramps, trouble breathing or a bite from a spider you suspect is dangerous. Children and older adults should be seen sooner. Call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) for advice.

## Ticks
- Use fine-tipped tweezers to grasp the tick close to the skin and pull straight up with steady pressure. Do not twist or crush it.
- Wash the area and your hands. Watch for fever, rash or flu-like illness over the following weeks and see a clinician if they appear.
- Prevent ticks with repellents, long clothing and tick checks after time outdoors.

!! Floodwater and cleanup bring snakes, rodents and insects into living spaces. Wear boots and gloves and watch where you reach.

# mental-health-crisis | Stress, Trauma and Mental Health Crises
category: medical
priority: high
tags: stress, trauma, mental health, anxiety, suicide, crisis, 988, grief, disaster stress, children
sources: cdc, red-cross, ready-gov
related: guide:children-preparedness, guide:community-resilience, guide:elderly-preparedness, guide:vulnerable-members
summary: Strong reactions after an emergency are common. Look after sleep, food and connection, watch for warning signs and call or text 988 or 911 if someone may be in danger.

## Overview
Disasters and emergencies are stressful. Many people feel anxious, sad, numb, angry, exhausted or unable to concentrate for days or weeks. Most recover with rest, support and routine. Some need professional help.

## Looking after yourself
1. Eat regular meals, drink water and sleep when you can.
2. Keep a simple routine and set small goals.
3. Stay connected with people you trust. Talk about how you feel.
4. Limit news and social media if they raise your distress.
5. Move your body: walking and stretching help.
6. Avoid using alcohol or drugs to cope.
7. Accept help and offer help. Doing something useful can restore a sense of control.

## Children and teens
Children may be clingy, afraid, irritable, have nightmares or return to earlier behaviors. Give simple, honest explanations, extra attention, routines and reassurance. Let them express feelings through talk or play. Seek help if distress lasts or interferes with life.

## Warning signs that someone needs help
Talk of wanting to die or hurt themselves, giving away belongings, hopelessness, severe changes in sleep or appetite, extreme agitation, confusion, inability to function, substance misuse, or violence toward others.

## What to do in a crisis
1. **If there is immediate danger, call 911.** Stay with the person.
2. In the U.S., **call or text 988**, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, 24 hours a day. You can also use it when you are worried about someone else.
3. Listen without judgment. Ask directly whether they are thinking about suicide. Asking does not put the idea in their mind.
4. Do not leave them alone. Help remove access to firearms, medications and other means where you can do so safely.
5. Encourage them to see a clinician or counselor, and go with them if you can.

## After a disaster
Many communities offer free crisis counseling. Ask your local health department, the Red Cross or a shelter staff member.

!! If you are not in the U.S., use your local emergency number and crisis services.

# emergency-childbirth | Emergency Childbirth
category: medical
priority: high
tags: childbirth, labor, delivery, pregnancy, newborn, baby, emergency birth, postpartum bleeding
sources: red-cross, cdc, ready-gov
related: guide:shock, guide:bleeding-control, guide:hypothermia, guide:cpr-aed
summary: Call 911 first. If the baby is coming before help arrives, stay calm, support the baby's head and keep mother and baby warm.

## Overview
Most births are not emergencies, but labor can move quickly, especially in a later pregnancy, and disasters can cut off access to care. Dispatchers are trained to coach you step by step. **Call 911 first** and keep the phone on speaker.

## Signs that birth is close
Contractions closer than about 2 minutes apart and lasting about a minute, a strong urge to push or bear down, a feeling of needing a bowel movement, the baby's head visible (crowning), or the water has broken and she cannot walk.

## If birth cannot wait
1. **Call 911.** Say that a birth is imminent.
2. Wash your hands if possible. Gather clean towels, blankets and a warm place. Help her into a comfortable position, such as lying back with knees up or on hands and knees.
3. Let the baby come on its own pace. **Do not pull** on the baby. Place a hand lightly on the head to guide it out slowly.
4. If the umbilical cord is around the neck and loose, slip it gently over the head. Do not pull if tight.
5. Support the head and body as they emerge. Babies are slippery.
6. Once born, place the baby face-up on the mother's chest or belly, skin to skin. **Dry the baby with a towel and cover** them and the mother with a blanket. Cover the baby's head. Warmth is very important.
7. Rub the baby's back gently. If the baby is not breathing, follow the dispatcher's instructions for newborn breaths and chest compressions.
8. **Do not cut the cord** unless the dispatcher tells you to, or help is delayed for a long time. Leave it attached.
9. The placenta will usually deliver within about an hour. Do not pull on it. Keep it in a bag or towel for the medical team.
10. Gently massage the lower belly (uterus) after the placenta to help control bleeding. Let the baby nurse if they can.

## Warning signs after birth
Heavy bleeding (soaking a pad in about 15 minutes), faintness, fever, severe pain, a baby who is blue, limp or not breathing well. Call 911 and treat for shock.

## Preparing
Pregnant people should know the plan for their delivery location and a backup. Keep medical records, a go-bag with newborn items and transport options in case of evacuation.

!! Do not try to delay birth. Do not let the mother go to the toilet if birth seems imminent. Never pull on the baby or the cord.
