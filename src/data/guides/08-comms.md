# weather-radio | NOAA Weather Radio and Emergency Radios
category: comms
priority: critical
tags: NOAA weather radio, emergency radio, AM FM, hand crank, SAME, alerts, NWR
sources: noaa, nws, ready-gov, fema
related: checklist:comms-kit, guide:tornadoes, guide:hurricanes
summary: A battery or crank radio with NOAA Weather Radio gives alerts and news when internet and cell service fail.

## Overview
NOAA Weather Radio (NWR) broadcasts National Weather Service warnings, watches and forecasts continuously, 24 hours a day, from transmitters across the U.S. It also carries non-weather emergency alerts. It works without internet or cell service.

## Choose a radio
- Look for **NOAA Weather Radio with SAME** (Specific Area Message Encoding) so you can program your county and receive only relevant alerts, and an **alert function** that sounds an alarm for warnings even when the volume is low.
- Power: battery, hand-crank, solar, USB charging, or several of these. Many also charge phones in a pinch.
- Features: AM/FM for local news and information, flashlight and USB output are nice extras.
- Weather band frequencies are seven channels between **162.400 and 162.550 MHz**. Most radios scan for the strongest one.

## Set up
1. Program your SAME county code (find it from NWS resources while online or in the manual).
2. Test the alert function monthly.
3. Keep spare batteries and rotate them.
4. Place the radio where you will hear it at night. Keep it plugged in or on a charger, with batteries as backup.

## Using AM/FM during emergencies
Local AM/FM stations are often the best source of local information and instructions. Know which stations serve your area. Tune to official emergency stations.

## Limits
Coverage depends on distance from a transmitter and terrain. In remote areas, reception can be poor. Warnings are most valuable when you can hear them at night.

>> A weather radio is one of the best low-cost investments for severe-weather safety.

# two-way-radios | Two-Way Radios (FRS, GMRS and CB)
category: comms
priority: normal
tags: two-way radio, walkie-talkie, FRS, GMRS, CB radio, short range, channel 9
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: guide:ham-radio-basics, guide:family-comms-plan, guide:comms-network-failure
summary: Short-range radios let family and neighbors talk when cellular networks are down, if you plan channels and test them.

## Overview
Two-way radios communicate directly without cell towers. Range is often much shorter than advertised: typically a few blocks to a couple of miles in real conditions, because of buildings and terrain.

## Types
- **FRS (Family Radio Service):** license-free handheld radios with low power. Good for neighborhoods and families.
- **GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service):** more power, longer range and repeaters. In the U.S. a license from the FCC is required; check the current rules and requirements on the FCC site.
- **CB (Citizens Band):** license-free, in cars and homes. Channel 9 is the traditional emergency channel and channel 19 is often used by truckers.
- **Marine VHF and other services:** for boaters, following their rules.

## Plan and practice
1. Buy radios of the same type for your group, with spare batteries or a charger.
2. Pick a primary and backup channel and write it down. Agree on check-in times.
3. Test them before you need them and learn the buttons.
4. Keep messages short, clear and without private information on an open channel.
5. Turn them off when not in use to save batteries.
6. Know the rules. Do not interfere with emergency traffic.

## Emergency uses
Coordinate with neighbors, check on each other, and share information. Do not rely on them as a substitute for official emergency services, and do not expect them to reach 911.

!! Radios that transmit can interfere if used improperly. Follow regulations.

# ham-radio-basics | Amateur (Ham) Radio Basics
category: comms
priority: normal
tags: ham radio, amateur radio, ARES, licensing, Technician, repeater, emergency communication
sources: ready-gov, fema
related: guide:two-way-radios, guide:comms-network-failure, guide:neighborhood-preparedness
summary: Ham radio operators provide long-range emergency communications. A license is required to transmit.

## Overview
Amateur radio is a hobby and a public service. During disasters, operators relay messages and support emergency agencies when other systems fail. Many volunteer through local emergency communication groups.

## Getting started
1. In the U.S., a license from the FCC is required to transmit. The entry-level license is the Technician class, which involves a written exam. Exams are given by volunteer examiners, and the process and fees are set by those organizations.
2. Find a local radio club for classes, help with equipment and nets (scheduled on-air meetings).
3. Start with an affordable handheld radio. Learn about repeaters, which extend range for handhelds.
4. Practice before disasters: join weekly nets, learn how to operate with battery or solar power.
5. Join an emergency communications group that serves your area for training and credentials.

## Emergency use
- Operators can relay health and welfare messages, report damage and provide communications for shelters and officials.
- Listening is generally permitted without a license, but transmitting is not.

## Limits
- Equipment needs power, antennas and skills.
- Do not transmit without a license, except in a genuine emergency when life or property is at stake, in which case the rules allow it, and still use the right procedures.

>> Even if you are not licensed, a shortwave or all-band receiver can provide news when local systems are down.

# family-comms-plan | Family Communication Plan and Contact Cards
category: comms
priority: critical
tags: family communication plan, contact card, check-in, text, out-of-area
sources: ready-gov, red-cross, fema
related: guide:contact-planning, guide:meeting-locations, guide:comms-network-failure
summary: Agree in advance how you will contact one another, in what order, and what to do if nothing works.

## Overview
You may be separated when a disaster strikes. A communication plan removes confusion. It works with the contact and meeting planning guides.

## Steps
1. Write a **family communication plan** that lists everyone's phone numbers, email, and workplace and school contacts.
2. Choose an **out-of-area contact**. Everyone calls or texts that person and reports where they are and how they are.
3. Decide **how** you will communicate: text first, then calls, then social media or messaging apps, then a meeting place.
4. Agree on **check-in times** such as every few hours or at a certain time each day.
5. Make **contact cards** for wallets, backpacks and go-bags, with the plan on the back. Include a child-friendly version.
6. Learn how **Wireless Emergency Alerts** work on your phone, and keep them enabled.
7. Know the **text-to-911** capabilities in your area. Call if you can, text if you can't, since not all areas support it.
8. Keep the plan updated and practice it.

## During the event
- Use text and data-based messaging rather than voice calls to avoid overloading networks.
- Keep messages short: "I'm OK at [location]. Will wait at [place]."
- Use social media check-in tools sparingly. Do not post exact locations or sensitive information.
- Register on the Red Cross Safe and Well site or similar services when available.

>> Practice sending a group text and a message to the out-of-area contact once per year.

# offline-maps-navigation | Offline Maps, Paper Maps and GPS
category: comms
priority: high

sources: ready-gov, nws, fema
related: guide:compass-basics, guide:fuel-and-routes, guide:evacuation-planning
summary: GPS receives satellite signals without internet. Map data must be downloaded in advance, and a paper map is the backup.

## Overview
GPS and mapping are often confused. They are different systems:
- **GPS** (and similar satellite systems) provides your position from signals sent by satellites. A GPS receiver does **not** need an internet or cell connection to know where you are.
- **Map data** (roads, places, trails) must be stored on your device. Apps that fetch maps over the internet will show a blank map without a connection unless you have downloaded the area ahead of time.

## Prepare before a disaster
1. Download **offline maps** for your home area, workplace, evacuation routes and destinations in a mapping app. Check how large each area is and how often it must be refreshed.
2. Keep a **paper road map or atlas** in each car and go-bag, plus local maps of your neighborhood.
3. Mark evacuation routes, shelters, hospitals, fuel stations, and meeting places on the paper map.
4. Practice using offline maps in airplane mode.
5. Consider a dedicated handheld GPS or a phone with a battery backup.

## Limits and tips
- GPS accuracy can be reduced by buildings, canyons, dense tree cover or interference. It can take longer to get a first fix without internet-assisted data.
- Offline map data can be out of date, especially if roads are flooded or closed after a disaster.
- Phones burn battery fast using GPS. Use airplane mode and low-power settings, and carry a power bank.
- Local conditions change. Follow instructions from officials and observe real roadblocks.

!! Do not rely on a single method. Combine offline maps, paper maps and a compass, and know your route.

# compass-basics | Compass and Navigation Basics
category: comms
priority: normal
tags: compass, magnetic north, declination, bearing, landmarks, map reading
sources: ready-gov, usgs
related: guide:offline-maps-navigation, guide:evacuation-planning
summary: Learn to read a map, take a bearing and use landmarks so you can navigate without electronics.

## Overview
A simple baseplate compass and a map allow you to navigate with no batteries. Practice before you need it.

## Key ideas
- A compass needle points to **magnetic north**, which differs from true (map) north by a **declination** angle that varies by location. Local maps and USGS resources list it.
- Keep the compass away from metal, magnets and electronics (cars, phones) that can deflect the needle.

## Steps to orient a map
1. Hold the compass flat. Rotate your body until the needle aligns with the orienting arrow (north).
2. Place the compass on the map with the edge along a north-south line. Turn the map and compass together until the needle lines up. The map is now oriented.
3. Identify landmarks around you and match them to the map.

## Taking a bearing to a destination
1. Place the long edge of the compass between your position and your destination on the map, with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing to the destination.
2. Rotate the compass dial so the orienting lines match the map's grid lines, with north on the dial pointing to map north.
3. Account for declination as instructed by the compass or map.
4. Hold the compass level and turn until the needle aligns, then walk in the direction of the travel arrow, picking landmarks to walk toward.

## Other tips
- Sun and shadows, and the position of the moon and stars, give rough directions: the sun rises in the east and sets in the west.
- Walk in a straight line to the next landmark and re-check often.
- Do not leave a safe place to navigate in a severe event unless you must.

>> Practice on a local park or familiar neighborhood so you build confidence before a crisis.

# phone-battery-conservation | Phone Battery Conservation
category: comms
priority: high
tags: phone battery, conservation, low power mode, airplane mode, power bank
sources: ready-gov, red-cross
related: guide:usb-phone-charging, guide:comms-network-failure
summary: Make your phone last days instead of hours with a few settings and habits.

## Overview
In an outage, you may not be able to recharge for days. Conserve power for essential uses, such as calls, texts and alerts.

## Steps
1. Turn on **Low Power Mode** or battery saver.
2. Lower screen brightness and shorten auto-lock time.
3. Turn on **Airplane Mode** when you do not need contact. If service is weak, the phone drains battery looking for a signal. Turn it off briefly to check messages at set times.
4. Close unused apps, turn off background refresh and location services you do not need.
5. Turn off Bluetooth and Wi-Fi unless needed.
6. Use text rather than voice or video calls.
7. Keep the phone warm in cold weather, since batteries lose capacity in the cold. Keep it in an inner pocket.
8. Use a battery-powered flashlight rather than your phone's.
9. Download offline maps, documents and music ahead of time.
10. Keep a spare power bank and cable.

## Emergency charging
Use power banks, a car charger (outdoors or in a ventilated area), solar panels or hand-crank devices. Share one charging station with neighbors.

## Plan
Set a schedule: for example, check messages on the hour and then switch to airplane mode.

>> Keep your phone's battery above 20–30% in a disaster, as you cannot predict when you will need it.

# comms-network-failure | Communicating When Cell Networks Fail
category: comms
priority: high
tags: cell outage, network failure, SMS, wireless emergency alerts, satellite, mesh, no service
sources: ready-gov, fema, red-cross
related: guide:family-comms-plan, guide:weather-radio, guide:two-way-radios
summary: Networks fail or overload. Know the backup options and their limits.

## Overview
During disasters, networks may be overloaded or physically damaged, and cell towers have limited backup power. Redundancy is the answer: have more than one way to get information and to contact people.

## Options in order of simplicity
1. **Text messages (SMS):** use less capacity than voice calls and often get through when calls fail. Messages may be delayed.
2. **Data messaging apps and email:** may work when voice does not, if data service is available.
3. **Landline phones:** corded phones work without household power on traditional lines. Phones that depend on a home router or VoIP need power.
4. **Wi-Fi calling:** needs internet.
5. **Satellite messaging:** certain phones and devices can send short messages by satellite where there is a clear view of the sky. These need a compatible device and sometimes a plan.
6. **Two-way and ham radios:** local communications that do not depend on a network.
7. **Radio broadcasts:** NOAA Weather Radio and AM/FM for one-way information.
8. **In-person plans:** meeting places and check-in points.

## Good habits
- Keep calls short. Free up lines for emergencies.
- Do not call 911 to report outages or ask for information. Use it for life-threatening situations only.
- Text 911 only if your area supports it and a voice call is not possible.
- Share a plan with family so they know what to expect if you cannot be reached.
- Conserve phone battery and keep chargers ready.

>> Test your backup methods with your family now, rather than discovering limitations in an emergency.
