# nuclear-explosion-fallout | Nuclear Explosion and Fallout
category: threats
priority: critical
tags: nuclear, nuclear explosion, fallout, radiation, blast, shelter, get inside stay inside stay tuned
sources: ready-gov, cdc, fema, epa
related: guide:radiological-incidents, guide:shelter-in-place-planning, guide:weather-radio, guide:unsafe-water-sources, guide:extended-power-outages, checklist:shelter-in-place
summary: A nuclear detonation is rare but survivable for many people. Take cover from the blast, then get inside, stay inside and stay tuned for instructions.

## Overview
A nuclear explosion produces a blinding flash, intense heat, a blast wave and, afterward, radioactive dust called **fallout**. The most useful public guidance is three short phrases: **Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned.** Most people far from the explosion are hurt by fallout, not by the blast, and sheltering well greatly reduces exposure. Radioactivity in fallout drops quickly over the first hours and days.

## If you see a flash or hear of an attack
1. Do **not** look at the fireball. Take cover immediately behind anything solid, or get low and cover your head.
2. If outdoors, lie flat on the ground and cover your head and neck. Stay down until the blast wave has passed.
3. When it is safe to move, get **inside the nearest sturdy building**. Go to the center of the building or underground. More thick material between you and the outdoors (brick, concrete, earth) means better protection.
4. Stay inside for **at least 24 hours** unless officials tell you otherwise. Fallout is most dangerous in the first hours.
5. **Stay tuned**: use a battery or crank radio, TV or phone alerts. Officials will say when to leave, where to go and which routes are safe.

## While sheltering
- Close doors and windows. Turn off fans and air conditioning that pull in outside air.
- If you were outside, remove your outer layer of clothing (this can remove most radioactive dust), place it in a sealed bag away from people, then wash exposed skin and hair gently with soap and water. Do not scrub or scratch. Do not use conditioner, which can bind particles to hair.
- Eat and drink only packaged food and water from sealed containers or covered storage. Do not eat or drink anything that was uncovered outdoors.
- Bring pets inside and wash them if they were out.
- Use text messages instead of calls. Call 911 only for life-threatening injuries.

## Injuries
Blast, burns and flying debris cause the first injuries. Give first aid for bleeding and burns. See the Bleeding Control and Burns guides.

## Leaving
Do not leave to drive away on your own. Roads jam, and cars give little protection. Move only when officials say it is safe or if your building is unsafe.

## Potassium iodide
Potassium iodide protects only the thyroid, and only from radioactive iodine. It does not protect against other radiation. Take it **only if public health officials advise it**, and follow their instructions. Do not take it on your own.

!! Do not go outside to look, to find family or to pick up belongings in the first hours. Shelter first. Reunite later using your family plan.

# radiological-incidents | Radiological Incidents: Nuclear Plant Accidents and Dirty Bombs
category: threats
priority: high
tags: radiation, radiological, nuclear power plant, dirty bomb, radioactive, contamination, potassium iodide, decontamination
sources: ready-gov, cdc, nrc, epa
related: guide:nuclear-explosion-fallout, guide:shelter-in-place-planning, guide:hazardous-materials, guide:weather-radio
summary: Radiation emergencies other than a detonation: how to shelter, decontaminate and follow official instructions.

## Understanding the threat
- **Nuclear power plant accidents** are rare and plants are heavily regulated. If a release occurs, officials set emergency zones and may order sheltering or evacuation.
- A **radiological dispersal device (dirty bomb)** uses conventional explosives to spread radioactive material. The explosion itself usually causes most of the harm. The radioactive material spreads over a limited area.
- **Lost or stolen radioactive sources** (for example from industry or medicine) are another rare risk.
Radiation cannot be seen, smelled or felt. You reduce exposure with three principles: **time** (less time near the source), **distance** (farther is better) and **shielding** (put material between you and the source).

## Preparing beforehand
1. If you live near a nuclear plant, learn your emergency zone, alert system and evacuation routes from your local emergency management office.
2. Keep a battery radio, 3 days of food and water and a 72-hour kit ready.
3. Learn how to shelter in place (see the Shelter-in-Place guide).
4. Ask local health officials about potassium iodide programs in your area. Do not take it unless officials advise it.

## During an incident
1. **Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.** Close windows and doors and shut off outside air.
2. Move to an interior room, a basement or the center of the building.
3. Follow official instructions about sheltering or evacuating. Evacuate only if told to.
4. If you may have been contaminated: remove outer clothing, bag it, and wash skin and hair with soap and water. Do not scrub.
5. Dirty bomb: move away from the blast site, cover your nose and mouth with a cloth, go inside, then decontaminate as above. Give first aid for injuries.

## Afterward
Follow official guidance on when to return, what to eat and drink, and how to clean. Do not use tap water or food if officials say not to. Seek medical care if told you may have been exposed, or for nausea and vomiting in the first hours after exposure, skin burns or reddening.

## Common mistakes
- Evacuating on your own into a plume.
- Taking potassium iodide without an official advisory.
- Going outside to look.
- Cleaning contaminated clothing in the home washing machine without guidance.

!! Radiation alone rarely causes immediate symptoms except at very high exposures. Attend to bleeding, burns and breathing first.

# hazardous-materials | Hazardous Materials and Chemical Releases
category: threats

summary: Chemical spills and releases can spread as gas or liquid. Get away, upwind and uphill, or get inside and seal up, as officials direct.

## Overview
Hazardous materials (hazmat) incidents come from transport accidents (trucks, trains, pipelines), industrial plants, storage sites and deliberate acts. Some chemicals are gases that drift with the wind. Others are liquids on the ground. You may be told to **shelter in place** or to **evacuate**. Follow the instruction for your area.

## Signs of a release
Unusual smells, a cloud, a haze or smoke, people coughing or with burning eyes, dead birds or insects, emergency vehicles, a hissing sound, an alert on your phone or radio.

## What to do
1. **Move away and upwind.** Go uphill and at right angles to the wind. Many gases are heavier than air and sink into low ground.
2. Do not touch spilled material or walk through it. Do not drive through a cloud or spill.
3. If told to **shelter in place**: go inside right away, close and lock windows and doors, turn off fans, heating and air conditioning, close vents and fireplace dampers, and go to an interior room, usually above ground for heavier-than-air gases. Seal gaps with damp towels or tape if told to. Listen to the radio.
4. If told to **evacuate**: go calmly using the routes officials give. Take medications and pets.
5. Call 911 and give your location and what you see. Call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) for exposure questions.

## If you were exposed
1. Remove contaminated clothing at once. Cut it off rather than pulling it over your head. Bag it and keep it away from others.
2. Wash your skin with soap and plenty of water for about 15 minutes. Flush eyes with clean water for at least 15 minutes. Remove contact lenses and do not put them back.
3. Seek medical care. Tell responders what you were exposed to if you know.

## Afterward
Do not return until officials say it is safe. Ventilate the building when told. Discard food that was exposed.

!! Do not try to help a person in a contaminated area unless you are trained and equipped. Hazmat teams handle rescues.

# gas-leak | Natural Gas and Propane Leaks
category: threats
priority: critical

related: guide:ventilation-co-safety, guide:earthquakes, guide:fire-prevention-escape, guide:returning-home
summary: If you smell gas, leave immediately and call from outside. Do not use switches, flames or phones indoors.

## Overview
Natural gas and propane are explosive. Utilities add a smell, often described as rotten eggs, so leaks can be noticed. Leaks follow earthquakes, floods, construction accidents, damaged appliances and corroded pipes.

## Signs
A rotten-egg or sulfur smell, a hissing or whistling sound near a gas line or appliance, bubbles in standing water, dead or dying plants near a buried line, or a damaged gas meter or pipe. Some people cannot smell the additive well, so do not rely on smell alone.

## What to do
1. **Leave immediately.** Take everyone, including pets, and go well away from the building, upwind.
2. **Do not** turn on or off lights or appliances, use a lighter or match, start a vehicle in an attached garage, or use a landline or ring a doorbell inside. A spark can ignite gas.
3. From a safe distance outside, **call 911 and your gas utility**.
4. Do not re-enter until the fire department or utility says it is safe.
5. If you know how and it is safe to reach, shut off the gas at the meter only if officials or the utility have taught you to. Only the utility should restore gas.
6. Seek medical care for dizziness, headache, nausea or fainting. These can be signs of gas exposure or carbon monoxide.

## After an earthquake or flood
Check for gas smell once you are safe. If you smell gas or hear a hiss, leave and call from outside. Do not light candles.

## Prevention
- Have fuel-burning appliances and lines inspected.
- Install carbon monoxide alarms and, where recommended, gas detectors.
- Know the location of your meter and the manufacturer's shutoff instructions.
- Call 811 before digging to locate buried lines.

!! Never look for a gas leak with a flame. If in doubt, leave and call.

# tsunamis | Tsunamis
category: threats
priority: critical
tags: tsunami, tidal wave, coast, high ground, warning, evacuate, earthquake, sea level
sources: noaa, nws, usgs, ready-gov
related: guide:earthquakes, guide:evacuation-planning, guide:go-bag, checklist:evacuation
summary: A tsunami is a series of dangerous waves. If you are on the coast and feel strong shaking, hear a warning or see the sea behave strangely, move inland and uphill on foot immediately.

## Understanding the threat
Tsunamis are caused by earthquakes, landslides and volcanic eruptions under or near the sea. They are not a single wave. A series of surges can arrive over hours, and the first may not be the largest. They can travel quickly and flood far inland.

## Preparing beforehand
1. If you live, work or play on the coast, learn whether you are in a tsunami hazard zone and where the nearest high ground is.
2. Learn your community's evacuation routes and signs. Practice walking them.
3. Keep a go-bag at the door and a family plan with a meeting place on high ground.
4. Enable emergency alerts on your phone and own a weather radio.

## Warning signs
- **Natural warnings** (act at once, do not wait for an alert): strong or long shaking (20 seconds or more), a sudden rise or fall of the sea, or a loud roar from the ocean.
- **Official alerts:** a tsunami warning means a dangerous tsunami is expected or occurring. A watch means one is possible.

## During the event
1. **Move immediately** inland and to higher ground on foot. Go as high and as far from the shore as you can. Many local plans point to ground at least 100 feet above sea level or about 2 miles (3 km) inland, but follow your local zone map and officials.
2. Do not wait for official confirmation if you feel the natural warnings.
3. Do not drive if you can walk: roads jam.
4. If you cannot reach high ground, go to an upper floor of a sturdy multi-story building as a last resort.
5. Stay away from the shore. Do not go to watch the waves.
6. Stay where you are until officials give the all-clear. Waves and strong currents can continue for many hours.

## Afterward
Avoid floodwater and debris, and stay away from damaged structures and downed lines. Treat tap water as unsafe until cleared. Be prepared for aftershocks.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting for a siren or confirmation.
- Returning to the shore after the first wave.
- Driving into the traffic.
- Going to look at the receding water.

!! If you are in a boat, follow harbor authorities and the Coast Guard's guidance. Do not return to port while a tsunami warning is in effect unless told to.

# volcanoes | Volcanic Eruptions and Ashfall
category: threats
priority: high
tags: volcano, volcanic ash, ashfall, lava, lahar, eruption, N95, evacuation
sources: usgs, ready-gov, cdc
related: guide:evacuation-planning, guide:shelter-in-place-planning, guide:flash-floods, guide:landslides
summary: Volcanic hazards include ash, lava, gases and mudflows. Follow evacuation orders, protect your lungs from ash and stay out of river valleys.

## Understanding the threat
Eruptions can send ash clouds high into the air, and ash can fall for hundreds of miles. Other hazards are fast hot flows of gas and rock, slow lava flows, harmful gases and **lahars**, fast-moving mud and debris flows in river valleys, which can happen long after an eruption.

## Preparing beforehand
1. If you live near a volcano, learn your hazard zones and evacuation routes from local officials and the USGS volcano observatory.
2. Sign up for local alerts.
3. Keep N95 respirators, goggles, sealed food and water, a battery radio and a go-bag ready.
4. Know how to protect air intakes and electronics.

## During ashfall
1. **Stay indoors.** Close windows, doors and chimney dampers. Turn off air conditioning and fans that draw in outside air. Seal gaps with damp towels.
2. If you must go out, wear an **N95 respirator** and goggles (not contact lenses) and cover your skin.
3. Avoid driving: ash reduces visibility and clogs engines. If you must, drive slowly with headlights on and change air filters often.
4. Keep pets and livestock inside and give them clean water and feed.
5. Protect electronics and covered water supplies.

## Evacuation
Leave when ordered. Avoid river valleys and low areas, where lahars flow. Go uphill and away from drainage paths. Take medications, documents and pets.

## Afterward
- Ash is heavy, especially when wet. Remove it from roofs gently with a broom, wearing a respirator, to prevent collapse. Do not wash it into storm drains.
- Wet ash slightly before sweeping to reduce dust.
- Do not return to evacuated areas until officials say it is safe. Eruptions can continue.

## Common mistakes
- Staying in a valley because the eruption "looks small".
- Running the car engine in thick ash.
- Wearing a cloth mask instead of an N95.

!! People with asthma, COPD or heart disease are at higher risk from ash. Stay inside and keep medications close.
